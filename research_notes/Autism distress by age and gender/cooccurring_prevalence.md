# Co-occurring issues that drive or look like distress in autistic people: prevalence by age and sex

Scope note: research notes for realistic synthetic data and red-flag rules in CareSignal. Not diagnostic. Medication, restraint, and clinician-supervised interventions are excluded; where relevant a topic is marked "for the care team".

Method note (read this first): in this session the hosts pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and stacks.cdc.gov were blocked, so full texts could not be opened. Every number below was taken from abstracts, publisher or repository listings, and press summaries returned by web search. Where a number rests on a secondary summary only, that is stated and the confidence is lowered. Confidence key: high = figure appears in the paper abstract or in several independent summaries that agree; medium = single abstract-level or reputable secondary source; low = secondary only, conflicting, or small sample.

## 1. Sleep: prevalence by age band, types, link from a poor night to next-day distress, sex differences

### Takeaway
Sleep problems are the most common co-occurring issue: roughly half of autistic preschoolers and about 70 percent or more of school-age autistic children by parent-report screening cutoffs, and they persist into adolescence and adulthood while changing form (night waking and bedtime resistance when younger; delayed sleep onset, short sleep, and daytime sleepiness when older). Night waking and irregular sleep are the features most consistently tied to next-day aggression, irritability, and self-injury, which supports a "poor night, then harder day" statistic in the app.

### Cited Findings

Prevalence by age
- Ages 2 to 5: 53 percent of autistic children had at least one frequent sleep problem, versus 46 percent of children with developmental delay and 32 percent of typically developing children. Two factors emerged: sleep onset problems and night waking; autistic children were significantly higher on night waking and marginally higher on sleep onset. Cognitive or adaptive level did not predict severity. Sample: CHARGE population-based case-control study, n=529 (303 autistic, 63 DD, 163 TD), mean age 3.6 years (Krakowiak et al. 2008, J Sleep Res). Confidence: high. [QxMD abstract](https://read.qxmd.com/read/18482108/sleep-problems-in-children-with-autism-spectrum-disorders-developmental-delays-and-typical-development-a-population-based-study)
- Ages 2 to 5: autistic children had about 2.1 times the odds of sleep problems versus children with other developmental delays and about 2.4 times versus the general population at a conservative CSHQ cutoff of 48 (adjusted OR 2.12 and 2.37); at the standard cutoff of 41 the odds ratio versus general population was about 1.45. Sample: multisite Study to Explore Early Development, n=1,987, parent-completed Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire (Reynolds et al. 2019, Pediatrics, doi 10.1542/peds.2018-0492). The authors linked sleep problems to anxiety. Confidence: high for odds ratios; the exact percentage per group was not retrievable. [MDedge summary](https://mdedge.com/content/high-prevalence-sleep-problems-children-autism-spectrum-disorder)
- Ages 4 to 10: parents reported significant sleep problems in 71 percent of children (CSHQ cutoff 41), while only 30 percent had a sleep diagnosis in the clinical record. Sample: Autism Treatment Network registry, n=1,518, 2009 to 2013 (Malow et al. 2016, Pediatrics supplement). The authors noted that cutoff 41 may be too low for autistic children, so 71 percent is an upper-range estimate. Confidence: medium (press summary of the paper). [MDedge summary](https://mdedge.com/content/sleep-problems-common-among-youth-asd)
- Ages 3 to 17 (childhood to adolescence): sleep problems were significant in every age group, but the type changed. Younger children had more bedtime resistance, sleep anxiety, parasomnias, and night wakings; older children and adolescents had more delayed sleep onset, shorter sleep duration, and daytime sleepiness. Sample: Autism Treatment Network, n=1,859, CSHQ (Goldman, Richdale, Clemons, Malow 2012, J Autism Dev Disord, doi 10.1007/s10803-011-1270-5). Confidence: high for the pattern; per-age-band percentages were not in the abstract. [Journal listing](https://mijn.bsl.nl/parental-sleep-concerns-in-autism-spectrum-disorders-variations-/552366)
- School age: clinically significant sleep problems affect up to 86 percent of the autistic school-age population (statement in Estes et al. 2022, Autism Research). Confidence: medium (upper bound quoted in an abstract). [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/36341856/)
- Adults (16 and older): a meta-analysis of eight datasets found that autistic adults, compared with non-autistic adults, had lower sleep quality and sleep efficiency, longer time to fall asleep, more time awake after sleep onset, and more napping; actigraphy showed more fragmented sleep (Morgan et al. 2020, Sleep Medicine). A second meta-analysis of 42 studies of autistic and/or ADHD adults reached the same direction (Ramos-Quiroga group, European Psychiatry 2021). No pooled percentage was retrieved. Confidence: high for direction, no prevalence figure. [Scinapse listing of Morgan et al.](https://scinapse.io/papers/2966550280); [European Psychiatry meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9471727/)

Poor night linked to next-day distress
- In 81 autistic children, sleep problems were significantly associated with physical aggression, irritability, inattention, and hyperactivity; night awakenings had the most consistently strong association with daytime behavior, after controlling for age and sex (Mazurek and Sohl 2016, J Autism Dev Disord, doi 10.1007/s10803-016-2723-7). Cross-sectional parent report, so association only. Confidence: medium (small sample, abstract level). [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/02/160216143912.htm); [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/sleepless-nights-rouse-behavioral-problems-children-autism/)
- Night-to-day prediction: across more than 20,000 nightly sleep observations from 67 autistic people with high support needs living in two US residential facilities, the previous night's sleep predicted next-day challenging behavior (aggression, self-injury, tantrums, property destruction) better than chance in 81 percent of individuals. Sleep variability (irregular timing and duration) was a stronger predictor than hours of sleep alone (Cohen et al. 2018, Autism Research 11:391 to 403, doi 10.1002/aur.1899). Confidence: medium to high for this residential population; generalization to children at home is an inference. [QxMD abstract](https://read.qxmd.com/read/29197172/sleep-patterns-predictive-of-daytime-challenging-behavior-in-individuals-with-low-functioning-autism)
- In 1,193 children aged 4 to 10 from 14 Autism Treatment Network centers, children with sleep problems had more internalizing and externalizing difficulties than those without (Sikora et al. 2012, Pediatrics supplement). Confidence: medium. [MDedge summary](https://mdedge.com/content/sleep-problems-common-among-youth-asd)
- A meta-analysis of 49 articles (all ages) found sleep problems significantly associated with more autism-related features and worse daytime functioning (Han et al. 2022). Confidence: medium (seen only as a search summary). [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10803-021-05382-1)
- In a study of 1,584 autistic children, physical aggression was more likely in children who also had self-injury, sleep problems, or sensory problems (Mazurek, Kanne, Wodka 2013; reported in press coverage). Confidence: medium. [Disability Scoop](https://www.disabilityscoop.com/?p=17510)

Sex differences
- School age: autistic girls showed more sleep problems than autistic boys and than typically developing girls and boys (Estes et al. 2022, Autism Research, "Sleep problems in autism: sex differences in the school-age population"). Confidence: medium. [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/36341856/)
- Saré and Smith 2020 reported that the correlates differ by sex: poor sleep tracked with more repetitive behavior in girls and with more social communication difficulty in boys. Confidence: low (secondary summary only). [Summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/association-between-sleep-deficiencies-with-behavioral-problems-in-aut/)
- Evidence is mixed: a study of infants and toddlers found no sex difference in sleep duration or efficiency, and a small accelerometer study (n=24) found lower sleep efficiency in girls. Confidence: low. [MDPI Clocks and Sleep](https://www.mdpi.com/2624-5175/6/4/49/htm)

### Inferences
- For synthetic data, reasonable "any notable sleep problem" base rates are about 50 percent at ages 2 to 5, 60 to 75 percent at ages 6 to 11, and a similar or slightly lower overall rate at 12 to 17 with the type shifting to late sleep onset and short sleep. These bands are my interpolation from the studies above, not published per-band figures.
- Give girls a modestly higher sleep-problem rate than boys (small effect), flagged as uncertain.
- The statistics engine should model three separate nightly features: night waking count, sleep onset delay, and night-to-night variability. The literature points at night waking and variability more than total hours.
- A correlational link is the honest framing: "harder days more often followed nights with waking" is supported; "poor sleep caused the meltdown" is not.

### Gaps
- Exact percentages per age band (2 to 5, 6 to 11, 12 to 17) from Goldman 2012 and Reynolds 2019 were not retrievable (full texts blocked).
- No pooled insomnia prevalence for autistic adults was found.
- No day-level (within-child, home setting) study in children was retrieved; Cohen 2018 is residential and mostly adolescents and adults.
- The AAP 2020 report is widely quoted as giving a 50 to 80 percent range for sleep problems; I could not verify that wording in this session.

## 2. Elopement (wandering or running off): prevalence, age curve, triggers and destinations, injury and drowning risk, sex differences

### Takeaway
About half of autistic children are reported to have eloped at least once after age 4, with the highest rate at ages 4 to 7 (46 percent) falling to 27 percent at ages 8 to 11, and about a quarter to a third of school-age children wandering in any given year. The main dangers are traffic and water; drowning dominates fatal outcomes. No reliable sex difference was found.

### Cited Findings
- 49 percent of parents reported that their autistic child had eloped at least once after age 4. Sample: Interactive Autism Network online parent survey, 1,218 autistic children and 1,076 non-autistic siblings (Anderson et al. 2012, Pediatrics 130(5):870 to 877, doi 10.1542/peds.2012-0762). Self-selected registry sample, so likely some upward bias. Confidence: high. [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/10/121008082650.htm); [CDC author manuscript](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/33848/cdc_33848_DS1.pdf)
- Age curve from the same study: 46 percent eloped at ages 4 to 7, about four times the sibling rate (one summary gives the sibling rate as 11 percent); 27 percent at ages 8 to 11 versus 1 percent of siblings. Confidence: high for the autistic rates, medium for the sibling comparison. [Disability Scoop](https://disabilityscoop.com/?p=16601); [Autism Speaks](https://www.autismspeaks.org/blog/study-confirms-autism-wandering-common)
- Among children who went missing: close calls with traffic injury for 65 percent and with drowning for 24 percent; children were missing for 41.5 minutes on average. Elopement rose with autism severity. 56 percent of parents called elopement one of the most stressful things they cope with. Confidence: high (denominator is children who went missing, not all autistic children). [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/nearly-half-of-children-with-autism-wander-from-families); [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/almost-half-children-autism-wander)
- Where from: almost three quarters of the time, elopement was from the child's own home or another home (friend or relative). Confidence: medium. [CHOP Research in Action](https://injury.research.chop.edu/blog/posts/wandering-kids-autism-spectrum-disorders)
- Motivations: the most common parent-reported reason was that the child simply enjoys running or exploring (54 percent). Confidence: medium (Child Mind Institute article; the figure matches the Anderson survey but the article does not name it). [Child Mind Institute](https://childmind.org/article/autism-plus-wandering-a-dangerous-combination/)
- Past-year prevalence, ages 6 to 17: 26.7 percent of children with autism, intellectual disability, or developmental delay had wandered in the previous year (range 22.7 to 34.6 percent by condition group), mostly from public places; ages 6 to 11 were more likely to wander than ages 12 to 17. Sample: 2011 Pathways survey, nationally representative, parents of children with special health care needs (Kiely et al. 2016, PLoS ONE e0148337). Confidence: high. [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/6915cfcc0b97479a86258d6d3da105b8)
- Same Pathways data: wandering in 37.7 percent of autistic children with intellectual disability, 32.7 percent of autistic children without, 23.7 percent with intellectual disability only; differences not statistically significant (Rice et al. 2016, J Pediatr). Confidence: high. [Pharmacy Times summary](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/news/parents-of-children-with-autism-face-challenges-with-wandering)
- Injury mortality: of deaths in people with an autism diagnosis on US death certificates 1999 to 2014, 27.9 percent were from injury (proportionate mortality ratio 2.93). For autistic children under 15 the unintentional injury PMR was 41.87. Drowning PMR 39.89 (n=74), suffocation 31.93, asphyxiation 13.50; these three accounted for 79.4 percent of injury deaths in autistic children. Mean age at injury death 29.1 years versus 54.7 in the general population (Guan and Li 2017, Am J Public Health). A PMR is a share-of-deaths measure, not an absolute risk ratio, and the authors say so. Confidence: high for the figures, with that caveat. [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20170328/patients-with-autism-at-significantly-higher-risk-for-injuryrelated-death); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388960/)
- National Autism Association review of 808 missing-person cases, 2011 to 2016: nearly a third ended in death or needed medical attention; accidental drowning was 71 percent of deaths and traffic injury 18 percent. An earlier NAA figure (2009 to 2011, ages 14 and under) put drowning at 91 percent of wandering deaths; do not mix the two. Media-report-based case series, not population data. Confidence: medium. [NAA](https://nationalautismassociation.org/?p=3822); [NAA AWAARE](https://awaare.nationalautismassociation.org/autism-wandering)
- CDC maintains a public wandering (elopement) safety page for families. Confidence: high. [CDC](https://cdc.gov/child-development/disability-safety/wandering.html)
- Sex: a secondary commentary quoting Kiely et al. reports no meaningful association of wandering with sex, income, or race or ethnicity. Confidence: low (blog quotation). [Commentary](https://questioning-answers.blogspot.ca/2016/02/wandering-and-autism-continued.html)

### Inferences
- Synthetic data: lifetime "has ever run off" near 45 to 50 percent, highest incidence at ages 4 to 7, tapering through 8 to 11 and lower again in teens; past-year rate about 25 to 35 percent at ages 6 to 17. Use the same rate for girls and boys.
- Useful log fields: place left from (home, school, public place), apparent goal (exploring or running, heading to a favorite place, getting away from noise or a demand), proximity to water or road.
- Any elopement with a near miss (water, traffic) or an injury fits the "injury" red flag. A child who is currently missing is an emergency (call 911), which is stronger than the care team prompt.

### Gaps
- The full Anderson motivation list (for example heading to a favorite place, escaping an anxious situation, escaping sensory discomfort) and the 12 to 17 rate could not be verified with percentages because the manuscript host was blocked.
- No sex-stratified elopement prevalence was found.
- No good data on elopement in autistic adults was found.

## 3. Self-injury (head banging, biting, hitting self): prevalence by age and sex, persistence, risk markers

### Takeaway
Self-injury is common: about 42 percent pooled across studies and about 28 percent in population-based record review of 8-year-olds. It decreases over time for most people but persists for roughly 44 percent across 10 years. Sex and age findings are inconsistent; the more reliable markers are co-occurring aggression, sleep problems, sensory differences, impulsivity and overactivity, and limited adaptive or communication skills.

### Cited Findings
- Pooled prevalence 42 percent (95 percent CI 38 to 47), 37 papers, 14,379 participants. By form: hand-hitting most common (23 percent), self-cutting least common (3 percent). Subgroup analyses found no association of overall prevalence with age, intellectual disability, or study quality. Females had higher prevalence than males (p = .013). Hair pulling and self-scratching were associated with intellectual disability (Steenfeldt-Kristensen, Jones, Richards 2020, J Autism Dev Disord 50:3857 to 3873). Confidence: high. [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7557528/); [University of Birmingham](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/the-prevalence-of-self-injurious-behaviour-in-autism-a-meta-analy)
- Population-based: self-injury documented for 27.7 percent of 8,065 autistic 8-year-olds in the CDC ADDM Network (2000, 2006, 2008 surveillance years), from health and education records reviewed by clinicians; varied by site. Clinic-based studies report more than 30 percent (Soke et al. 2016, J Autism Dev Disord 46:3607 to 3614). Records-based, so probably an undercount. Confidence: high. [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/45225); [Drexel record](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Brief-Report-Prevalence-of-Self-injurious-Behaviors/991019168695104721)
- Associated factors in two national samples (ADDM and SEED): self-injury was associated with about a dozen features including aggression, anxiety, hyperactivity, mood problems, and sleep problems; no link was seen with sex, race or ethnicity, digestive problems, or parental age (Soke et al. 2017, J Autism Dev Disord 47:285 to 296, doi 10.1007/s10803-016-2951-x; as reported by The Transmitter). Confidence: medium (press summary). [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/large-study-shows-self-injury-common-among-children-autism/)
- Preschool age: in SEED, autistic preschoolers had about twice the odds of self-injury compared with children with other developmental delays (aOR 2.13, 95 percent CI 1.53 to 2.97) (Soke et al. brief report). Descriptive SEED table: GI problems in 83 of 194 children with current self-injury (43 percent) versus 139 of 498 without (28 percent), unadjusted. Confidence: medium. [Journal listing](https://antoniusacademie.mijn.bsl.nl/brief-report-self-injurious-behaviors-in-preschool-children-with/15458180); [PMC table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5912993/table/T1)
- Persistence: self-injury persisted in 44 percent of individuals over 10 years. Sample: carers of 67 autistic people, questionnaire at baseline, 3 years (n=46), and 10 years. Impulsivity and overactivity were risk markers for persistence; a model using baseline impulsivity, interest and pleasure, stereotyped behavior, social communication, and adaptive functioning predicted self-injury at 10 years (Laverty et al. 2020, Molecular Autism). Confidence: medium (small cohort). [University of Birmingham](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/persistence-and-predictors-of-self-injurious-behaviour-in-autism-); [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/long-term-study-tracks-persistence-of-self-harm-in-autistic-people/?fspec=1)
- Sex conflict: the meta-analysis found higher prevalence in females [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7557528/); contradicted by Soke 2017, which found no sex association [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/large-study-shows-self-injury-common-among-children-autism/). Confidence in any sex effect: low.
- Pain as a driver: NICE consultation material notes that people with communication difficulties may express pain or discomfort through aggression or self-harming, and that physical problems are harder to identify in this group. Confidence: high as guidance. [NICE CG170 consultation responses](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/documents/autism-management-of-autism-in-children-and-young-people-consultation-comments-table-and-responses2)
- Self-harm more broadly: autistic people had about 3 times the odds of self-harm compared with non-autistic people (Blanchard et al. 2021, JAMA Network Open meta-analysis). This covers self-harm in the mental health sense, which overlaps with but is not the same as repetitive self-injury. Confidence: medium. [Mental Elf summary](https://www.nationalelfservice.net/learning-disabilities/autistic-spectrum-disorder/self-harm-autism-higher-risk/)

### Inferences
- Synthetic data: 25 to 30 percent of school-age children with any self-injury is the conservative, population-based setting; 40 percent fits a sample weighted toward children with higher support needs. Hitting self with a hand should be the most common form, then head banging and biting.
- Do not build a strong sex or age gradient into self-injury; the evidence does not support one.
- Make self-injury co-vary in the synthetic logs with poor sleep, sensory overload, and days with possible pain signs, since those are the supported correlates.
- New-onset self-injury, or self-injury aimed at one body area (ear, jaw, belly, head), is a sensible "signs of pain or illness" flag. This targeting idea is clinical lore reflected in the Buie consensus; I did not retrieve a quantitative study for it.

### Gaps
- Per-form pooled percentages other than hand-hitting (23 percent) and cutting (3 percent), for example head banging and self-biting, were not retrievable.
- Soke 2016 and 2017 adjusted odds ratios (including for limited speech, IQ, and sex) were not retrievable.
- Soke 2017 reported no link with GI problems while the SEED preschool table shows a raw difference; unresolved without full text.
- No age-banded prevalence (2 to 5, 6 to 11, 12 to 17) for self-injury was found; the meta-analysis found no age effect.

## 4. Pain and GI: prevalence, and evidence that pain shows up as behavior

### Takeaway
GI symptoms are several times more common in autistic children than in peers (pooled odds ratio about 3.6 to 4.4; constipation in about a third or more), and consensus guidance states that behavior such as aggression, agitation, self-injury, or disrupted sleep may be the main or only sign of a GI or other painful condition in a child with limited speech. Behavioral markers are common and non-specific, so they justify "possible things to check" and a care team prompt, never an inference of cause.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis (15 studies, 2,215 children): autistic children versus comparison children had higher odds of any GI symptom (OR 4.42, 95 percent CI 1.90 to 10.28), constipation (OR 3.86, 2.23 to 6.71), diarrhea (OR 3.63, 1.82 to 7.23), and abdominal pain (OR 2.45, 1.19 to 5.07) (McElhanon et al. 2014, Pediatrics 133(5):872 to 883). Confidence: high. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24777214/)
- Updated meta-analysis: total GI symptom prevalence 55 percent in autistic children, overall OR 3.64 versus typically developing children; constipation in 37 percent (Gan et al. 2023, Frontiers in Pediatrics). Confidence: medium to high. [Frontiers](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fped.2023.1120728/full)
- Chronic GI problems (lasting 3 months or more: constipation, abdominal pain, bloating, diarrhea, or nausea) in 24 percent of 2,973 autistic children aged 2 to 17 in the Autism Treatment Network. Anxiety and sensory over-responsivity each independently predicted chronic GI problems (Mazurek et al. 2013, J Abnorm Child Psychol 41:165 to 176, doi 10.1007/s10802-012-9668-x). Confidence: high. [Journal listing](https://mijn.bsl.nl/anxiety-sensory-over-responsivity-and-gastrointestinal-problems-/540900); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/809680)
- Clinic sample of 340 autistic children and adolescents aged 2 to 18: constipation 65 percent, stomachaches 47.9 percent, diarrhea 29.7 percent, nausea 23.2 percent. Ages 2 to 5: aggressive behavior was associated with upper GI symptoms (about 11.2 percent more likely to have nausea). Ages 6 to 18: internalizing patterns (anxiety, somatic complaints) were associated with GI symptoms (about 11.4 percent more likely to have nausea and 11.5 percent more likely to have stomachaches). Correlational (Ferguson, Dovgan, Takahashi, Beversdorf 2019, Frontiers in Psychiatry, doi 10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00194). Confidence: medium (single clinic; high rates reflect referral population). [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00194); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/06/190627133937.htm)
- ADDM 8-year-olds: unusual sleeping or eating habits and oppositional behavior were significantly associated with documented GI problems. Children with sleep abnormalities had a documented GI history in 11 percent versus 3.6 percent of those without. The authors caution that these behaviors were frequent in children with and without GI problems, so they have limited value as a screen (Maenner et al. 2012, J Autism Dev Disord, doi 10.1007/s10803-011-1379-6). Confidence: high for the association, with that explicit caution. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10803-011-1379-6)
- Consensus report: problem behavior may be the primary or sole symptom of an underlying medical condition, including some GI disorders; autistic people deserve the same thoroughness of GI workup as anyone else; careful evaluation matters because many children are nonverbal; behavioral support may complement but should not replace medical evaluation. News summaries list agitation, aggression, and sleep disturbance as ways GI discomfort shows up (Buie et al. 2010, Pediatrics 125 Suppl 1, doi 10.1542/peds.2009-1878C). The panel also stated that a GI disorder unique to autism has not been established. Confidence: high as expert consensus (not prevalence data). [University of Kentucky record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/evaluation-diagnosis-and-treatment-of-gastrointestinal-disorders-/); [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/consensus-evidence-autism-diets-lacking)
- A later review lists behaviors hypothesized to reflect GI distress: sleep disturbance, repetitive behaviors, self-injury, aggression, oppositional behavior, irritability or mood change, and tantrums. Confidence: medium. [Springer review](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40489-013-0007-0.pdf)
- NICE: before other approaches to behavior that challenges, assess factors that may trigger or maintain it, including physical disorders; coexisting physical conditions named include pain and gastrointestinal disorders. Confidence: high. [NICE QS51 statement 7](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs51/chapter/Quality-statement-7-Assessing-possible-triggers-for-behaviour-that-challenges)

### Inferences
- Synthetic data: constipation is the single most realistic recurring physical issue (roughly 25 to 40 percent of children); any GI symptom about 45 to 55 percent; chronic GI problems about a quarter.
- Age pattern worth encoding from Ferguson: in younger children (2 to 5) possible GI discomfort tends to sit alongside outward distress and aggression; in older children it sits alongside worry and body complaints.
- Log fields that make pain visible without diagnosing: days since last bowel movement, food refusal, touching or guarding a body part (ear, jaw, belly), change in sleep, fever. The app should phrase any link as "possible things to check".
- Because Maenner shows these markers are non-specific, the statistics should require a repeated pattern before surfacing it, and the copy should not name a cause.

### Gaps
- Dental pain and ear infection: I found no retrievable quantitative study on how often these present as self-injury or aggression in autistic children. Support is guideline and consensus level only (NICE and Buie). Treat as low-confidence clinical consensus.
- Sex differences in GI prevalence: none found.
- Reflux prevalence specifically was not retrieved.
- Age-banded GI prevalence beyond the Ferguson split was not found.
- Buie 2010 wording on self-injury specifically could not be confirmed from the retrieved summaries.

## 5. Food selectivity and food refusal by age

### Takeaway
Feeding problems are about five times more likely in autistic children than in peers, with food selectivity reported in roughly 45 percent or more; selectivity tends to ease with age but often persists.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of 17 prospective comparison studies: autistic children had significantly more feeding problems (standardized mean difference 0.89; OR 5.11, 95 percent CI 3.74 to 6.97) (Sharp et al. 2013, J Autism Dev Disord). Confidence: high. [Emory news](https://news.emory.edu/stories/2013/02/autism_nutritional_deficits/index.html); [Journal listing](https://antoniusacademie.mijn.bsl.nl/feeding-problems-and-nutrient-intake-in-children-with-autism-spe/555696)
- Ages 3 to 11: autistic children refused 41.7 percent of foods offered versus 18.9 percent for typically developing children (53 autistic, 58 comparison) (Bandini et al. 2010). Confidence: medium (taken from a secondary table). [PMC table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8323334/table/table2-1362361321995631)
- Change with age: in follow-up of the same cohort, high food selectivity fell from 83 percent to 44 percent between time points (Bandini et al. 2017). Confidence: low to medium (secondary citation; the 83 percent baseline likely refers to a selected subgroup). [PMC table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8323334/table/table2-1362361321995631)
- Food selectivity affects about 17 percent of children generally and above 45 percent of autistic children (Schmitt et al. 2008, as cited in a 2018 meta-analysis). Confidence: low (secondary citation). [Sage listing](https://https-sage-cnpereading-com-443.webvpn1.xju.edu.cn/doi/10.1177/1362361318794179)
- The AAP 2020 clinical report names feeding disorders and food selectivity among conditions clinicians should watch for in autistic children. Confidence: high. [AAP HealthyChildren](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/news/Pages/Identification-Evaluation-Management-of-Children-with-Autism.aspx); [URMC](https://www.urmc.rochester.edu/news/story/new-autism-guidelines-recommend-early-screening-treatment-for-developmental-delays-before-official-diagnosis)

### Inferences
- Synthetic data: selective eating in roughly half of younger children (2 to 7), easing to about a third by adolescence. This is an interpolation, not a published curve.
- Long-standing selectivity is a need to work with, not a red flag. A sudden new refusal of previously accepted foods or drinks is different: it fits "signs of pain or illness" (dental, throat, GI) and "sudden change".

### Gaps
- No age-banded prevalence table for food selectivity was retrievable.
- No sex difference data found.

## 6. Seizure onset peaks, as a reason to flag "sudden change"

### Takeaway
Epilepsy affects roughly 1 in 8 autistic children overall and about 1 in 4 by ages 13 and older, with onset described as peaking in the first years of life and again around early adolescence. That supports routing any sudden change (staring spells, new unexplained falls, loss of skills, abrupt behavior change) to the care team, especially in teens.

### Cited Findings
- Average epilepsy prevalence 12.5 percent in autistic children aged 2 to 17, rising to 26 percent at age 13 and older. Children aged 10 or older had 2.35 times the odds of an epilepsy diagnosis versus younger children. Only age and cognitive ability were independently associated with epilepsy. Sample: pooled cross-sectional data from several large US datasets (Viscidi et al. 2013, PLoS ONE, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0067797). Confidence: high. [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/30c78a374d1842959a43c46016eca262); [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/11717673/3701630.pdf?sequence=1)
- Two peaks: most childhood epilepsy begins in the first three years of life, and autistic children show a second rise in early adolescence. Confidence: medium (secondary coverage of the literature). [The Transmitter](https://thetransmitter.org/spectrum/risk-of-epilepsy-in-autism-tied-to-age-intelligence)
- Sex: earlier studies reported higher epilepsy rates in autistic females than males, but in Viscidi the multivariate model retained only age and cognitive ability. Confidence: low for a sex effect. [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/11717673/3701630.pdf?sequence=1)
- The AAP 2020 clinical report lists seizures among co-occurring medical conditions clinicians should be aware of. Confidence: high. [AAP HealthyChildren](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/news/Pages/Identification-Evaluation-Management-of-Children-with-Autism.aspx)

### Inferences
- The app should not try to identify seizures. It should treat "sudden major change" as a red flag at any age, with the copy "Please contact your doctor or care team". A first-ever convulsion, a seizure lasting several minutes, or a person who does not wake afterwards is an emergency (call 911); this is standard first-aid guidance that I did not source in this session.
- Synthetic data should not include seizure events as routine log entries; at most one scripted red-flag example.

### Gaps
- Exact onset-age distributions were not retrieved.
- No sourced list of seizure-like signs for caregivers was collected (outside scope: for the care team).

## 7. Which of these warrant "contact your doctor or care team" in a consumer app (AAP, NICE)

### Takeaway
Guidance from NICE and the Buie consensus converges on one rule: when behavior changes or escalates, look for a physical cause (pain, GI, illness) first, and involve health professionals. AAP directs clinicians to watch for sleep, feeding, GI, and seizure conditions. That maps cleanly onto CareSignal's existing red flags.

### Cited Findings
- NICE quality standard QS51 statement 7 (from CG170 and CG142): before starting other interventions for behavior that challenges, assess for factors that may trigger or maintain it, including physical disorders, the physical environment (including sensory factors), and the social environment. Pain and gastrointestinal disorders are named as coexisting physical conditions. Confidence: high. [NICE QS51](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs51/chapter/Quality-statement-7-Assessing-possible-triggers-for-behaviour-that-challenges)
- NICE CG170 says to assess factors that may increase the risk of behavior that challenges in routine assessment and care planning, including absence of predictability and structure and inadvertent reinforcement. Confidence: medium (search summary of the recommendations page). [NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/Recommendation)
- NICE guideline group acknowledged that communication-impaired people may express pain or discomfort through aggression or self-harming and that this is covered in section 1.4 of CG170. Confidence: high. [NICE consultation responses](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/documents/autism-management-of-autism-in-children-and-young-people-consultation-comments-table-and-responses2)
- Buie et al. 2010 consensus: behavior may be the primary or sole symptom of an underlying medical condition; behavioral approaches alone are not enough when a medical cause is possible. Confidence: high. [University of Kentucky record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/evaluation-diagnosis-and-treatment-of-gastrointestinal-disorders-/)
- AAP 2020 clinical report (Hyman, Levy, Myers; Pediatrics 145(1):e20193447): primary care should know about and address co-occurring sleep and feeding disorders, GI symptoms, obesity, seizures, ADHD, and anxiety. Confidence: high. [AAP HealthyChildren](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/news/Pages/Identification-Evaluation-Management-of-Children-with-Autism.aspx); [AAP report PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/ID.Eval.Mgt.Children.ASD(AAP.2020).pdf)
- Soke et al. 2016 recommend that clinicians ask about self-injury during assessments of autistic children. Confidence: high. [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/45225)
- CDC publishes wandering safety guidance for families, which signals that elopement is a recognized safety topic to raise with professionals. Confidence: high. [CDC](https://cdc.gov/child-development/disability-safety/wandering.html)

### Inferences
Proposed mapping for CareSignal rules (my synthesis, not a quotation of any guideline):
- Contact your doctor or care team, shown first: any self-injury that is new, more frequent, more intense, or leaves a mark; any injury; signs of pain or illness (guarding or hitting one body area, fever, vomiting, no bowel movement for several days, blood in stool, new refusal of food or drink, pulling at an ear, mouth or jaw distress); sudden major change in behavior, sleep, skills, or alertness; elopement with a near miss.
- Call 911 now: a child or adult who is missing and cannot be found quickly, especially near water or roads; serious fall or head injury; a first seizure-like event or one that does not stop; stroke signs.
- Worth mentioning to the care team at the next visit (not a red flag): long-running sleep problems (several weeks), long-standing selective eating, recurring constipation, repeated wandering attempts without incident.
- Never output: a cause, a diagnosis, a medication or supplement, or a restraint suggestion. Sleep aids and seizure or GI treatments are for the care team.

### Gaps
- The exact text of CG170 section 1.4 and of the AAP 2020 sections on wandering and on evaluating behavior change was not retrievable; wording above is from NICE quality-statement pages and summaries.
- No guideline was found that sets numeric thresholds for consumer apps (for example "after N nights of poor sleep"); thresholds would be a product decision.
- Autistica and Autism Speaks ATN toolkit content was not reviewed in this session.
