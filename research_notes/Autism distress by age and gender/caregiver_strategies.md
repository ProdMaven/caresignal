# Home-usable caregiver strategies for reducing distress in autistic children, adolescents, and adults: evidence by age and by sex or gender

Scope note: non-clinical, home-usable strategies only. Medication, supplements, restraint, seclusion, deprivation, and clinician-supervised procedures are excluded. Language follows "autistic child", "child", "autistic person" and frames strategies as meeting needs and reducing triggers.

Method note and access limits: 27 search and fetch calls were made on 2026-11-14. Direct fetches of ncaep.fpg.unc.edu, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, and www.nice.org.uk failed (hosts not reachable from this session), so findings for NCAEP 2020, NICE CG170, and several PubMed Central papers rest on search-result summaries of abstracts and secondary pages, not on full text. Where a number could not be verified against the primary source it is listed under Gaps, not under Cited Findings. Confidence labels: high = RCT, meta-analysis, or guideline confirmed in more than one source; medium = systematic review of single-case studies, single RCT, or abstract-level only; low = qualitative, practice guidance, single small study, or secondary summary.

## 1. Evidence level for each strategy and the ages it was studied in

### Takeaway
The best-supported everyday strategies are visual supports, antecedent (trigger-reducing) changes, parent-delivered routines, AAC, and exercise or movement, all listed among the 28 NCAEP 2020 evidence-based practices for ages 0 to 22, with most studies in preschool and primary-school ages. Low-arousal approach, quiet space, decompression after school, snack or drink, energy accounting, and period preparation rest on practice guidance and qualitative research, not trials.

### Cited Findings

Overall evidence frameworks
- NCAEP 2020 (Steinbrenner, Hume, et al.) reviewed studies from 1990 to 2017: 545 new studies plus 427 from the previous review, 972 articles in total, yielding 28 evidence-based practice categories. Coverage is birth to age 22, so it says nothing direct about adults over 22. Study type: systematic review of group and single-case designs. Confidence: high. [NCAEP 2020 report](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/EBP-Report-2020.pdf); [FPG executive summary](https://fpg.unc.edu/publications/executive-summary-evidence-based-practices-children-youth-and-young-adults-autism)
- Sex or gender of participants was reported in 93% of studies in the 2012 to 2017 review period, but the male to female split was not retrievable in this session. Confidence: medium. [NCAEP executive summary](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/sites/ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/files/imce/documents/EBP%20Executive%20Summary.pdf)
- NICE CG170 (autism in under 19s, support and management; 2013, updated 2021, reviewed July 2026) has a section 1.4 on anticipating and preventing "behaviour that challenges" and a section 1.6 on interventions that should not be used. NICE quality standard QS51 statement 7, citing CG170 recommendations 1.4.1 to 1.4.3, says autistic people who develop such behaviour should be assessed for possible triggers, including physical health conditions, mental health problems, and environmental factors. NICE stated in July 2024 that it plans to update the psychosocial intervention recommendations. Study type: national guideline. Confidence: high for structure, medium for wording (full text not fetched). [NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170); [NICE QS51 statement 7](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs51/chapter/Quality-statement-7-Assessing-possible-triggers-for-behaviour-that-challenges)

Visual schedules and visual supports
- Visual Supports is an NCAEP 2020 evidence-based practice, supported by 104 single-case design studies and 2 group design studies. Confidence: high. [AFIRM Visual Supports brief packet](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Visual-Supports-Brief-Packet.pdf)
- NCAEP age bands for visual supports as summarised by the Texas autism resource: evidence across 0 to 5, 6 to 14, and 15 to 22, with some outcome areas (academic, distress behaviour, cognitive, communication) supported only in certain bands. Confidence: medium (secondary summary). [Texas Autism Resource Guide: visual supports](https://www.txautism.net/interventions/visual-supports)
- Knight et al. 2015 systematic review: visual activity schedules can be considered an evidence-based practice for autistic individuals, especially when combined with systematic teaching procedures. Study type: systematic review of single-case studies. Confidence: medium. [Knight et al. 2015](https://cbmt.franciscan.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/32/2021/07/Knight-et-al.-2015_Visual-Schedules.pdf)
- Lequia et al. 2012 systematic review: 18 studies; activity schedules reduced distress behaviour in every reviewed study regardless of schedule form. Narrative review, no pooled effect size. Confidence: medium. [Lequia et al. 2012, ERIC EJ947663](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ947663)

Transition warnings, timers, first-then boards
- Dettmer et al. 2000: two elementary-age autistic boys, reversal design across home and community settings. Visual supports shortened the time between an instruction and starting the next activity and reduced the verbal and physical prompts needed for one child. Study type: single-case, n = 2. Confidence: low to medium. [Dettmer et al. 2000](https://scilit.net/publications/29c92521d8c772b7c8ed4d89d3db5066)
- Mouzakes and Thompson 2025 review: the included studies gave insufficient evidence that the visual schedule component itself was what improved transitions. Study type: systematic review. Confidence: medium. [Mouzakes and Thompson 2025](https://digitalcommons.wne.edu/casfacpubs/10/)
- Practice guidance notes individual variation: a visual timer or countdown helps some children, while others become more anxious when they can see exactly how long remains; ending at a natural stopping point can help. Study type: practice guidance. Confidence: low. [Autism New Zealand screen time guide](https://autismnz.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Screen-time-1.pdf)

Sensory tools (ear defenders, fidgets)
- Pfeiffer et al. 2019: six autistic children with sound sensitivity aged 8 to 16; skin conductance level and response frequency fell significantly between baseline and the first headphone phase, for both in-ear and over-ear types. Study type: single-subject multi-treatment, n = 6. Confidence: low to medium. [Pfeiffer et al. 2019, PubMed 31798424](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31798424/)
- A 2018 qualitative study reported that participants identified noise-attenuating headphones as increasing participation at home, in the community, and at school. Confidence: low. [Noise-attenuating headphones and participation, 2018](https://read.qxmd.com/read/30265827/impact-of-noise-attenuating-headphones-on-participation-in-the-home-community-and-school-for-children-with-autism-spectrum-disorder)
- NCAEP's sensory companion paper states the review found insufficient evidence for sensory diets or any other sensory-based practice (as distinct from clinician-delivered Ayres Sensory Integration, which is out of scope here). Confidence: medium. [NCAEP sensory integration companion](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/sites/ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/files/resources/Sensory%20Integration_A%20Companion%20to%20the%20NCAEP%20Report.pdf)

Deep pressure and weighted blankets or vests
- See section 2. Weighted blankets did not improve sleep in an RCT; weighted vests are not an evidence-based practice.

Movement or break time
- Exercise and Movement is one of the NCAEP 2020 evidence-based practices. Confidence: high. [NCAEP 2020 report](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/EBP-Report-2020.pdf)
- Teh et al. 2022 meta-analysis: 22 studies, 274 autistic children; exercise reduced stereotyped motor behaviours with Hedges' g = 1.16, shrinking to g = 0.51 under a more conservative between-case model. Higher intensity predicted larger effects; age, duration, and setting did not. Confidence: medium to high. [Teh et al. 2022](https://mijn.bsl.nl/effects-of-physical-exercise-interventions-on-stereotyped-motor-/19346472)
- Tarr et al. 2020 small-n meta-analyses: overall effect of exercise on stereotypic behaviours d = -0.463 and d = -0.456 across two analyses. Confidence: medium. [Tarr et al. 2020](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100097028954_0x000001)
- Elliott et al. 1994: six autistic adults with moderate to profound intellectual disability; only vigorous antecedent aerobic exercise reduced stereotypic and distress behaviours, less vigorous activity did not. Study type: single-case, n = 6. One of the few adult data points. Confidence: low. [Summary of Elliott et al. 1994](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/vigorous--aerobic-exercise-versus-general-motor-training-activities--e/)

Predictable routines and preparing for change (social narratives)
- Social Narratives is an NCAEP 2020 evidence-based practice. Confidence: high. [NCAEP 2020 report](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/EBP-Report-2020.pdf)
- Kokina and Kern 2010 meta-analysis of single-case studies: stories read just before the target situation, and stories about a single situation instead of a complex chain, were more effective. A secondary source reports about 51% of included studies showed benefit. Confidence: medium. [Kokina and Kern 2010, ASHA evidence map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/b8a776ce-b372-40f9-8f48-7659cbe61c75)
- ASSSIST-2 (Wright et al. 2024): cluster RCT, 87 UK primary schools, 249 autistic children. Primary outcome (teacher-rated SRS-2 at 6 months) difference -1.61 points, 95% CI -4.18 to 0.96, p = 0.220, not significant. Children who received at least six sessions showed a 3.37 point reduction. Study type: cluster RCT, primary-school age, school-delivered. Confidence: high. [ASSSIST-2, White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/216811/)

Choice-making
- Shogren et al. 2004 meta-analysis: 13 single-case studies, 30 participants with disabilities (not autism only); offering choice gave clinically significant reductions in distress behaviour. Confidence: medium. [Shogren et al. 2004](https://vufind.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c339t-58c543743509d93658701567e03650c4dd5bed494745628a448050c8cc5a48583)
- A 2018 review describes choice (including antecedent choice between tasks) as a non-invasive option that may help some individuals, and notes that which populations benefit most still needs study. Confidence: medium. [Choice review 2018](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40489-018-00154-7)

AAC supports at home
- Augmentative and Alternative Communication is one of five new evidence-based practice categories in NCAEP 2020. Confidence: high. [CAPTAIN summary of NCAEP EBPs](https://www.captain.ca.gov/documents/WHAT%20ARE%20EBPs%20for%20ASD%20CAPTAIN%20APRIL%202020%20FINAL%20FINAL.pdf)
- A 2018 meta-analysis of single-case studies rated high-tech speech-generating devices as having a moderate level of evidence for autistic children. Confidence: medium. [JSLHR 2018](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2018_JSLHR-L-17-0132)

Bedtime routine and sleep hygiene (non-medication)
- Malow et al. 2014: 80 autistic children aged 2 to 10 with sleep onset delay; parents randomised to group or individual sleep education. Sleep latency (actigraphy), insomnia subscales of the Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire, and child and family functioning measures improved one month after education; delivery format made no difference. No untreated control arm. Study type: RCT of delivery format. Confidence: medium. [Malow et al. 2014](https://emmes.com/publications/14139)
- Adkins et al. 2012: 36 autistic children with actigraphy-confirmed sleep latency of 30 minutes or more; a sleep education pamphlet alone did not improve sleep latency compared with no pamphlet. Study type: RCT. Confidence: medium. [Vanderbilt news on the pamphlet and education trials](https://news.vumc.org/2012/11/01/studies-explore-best-ways-to-manage-insomnia-in-autism/)

Low-arousal approach and quiet space
- McDonnell et al. 2015 is a theoretical paper proposing low arousal as a general strategy; the underlying hyperarousal premise has mixed experimental support. Confidence: low. [Summary of McDonnell et al. 2015](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/the-role-of-physiological-arousal-in-the-management-of-challenging-beh/)
- The strongest published outcome evidence is a 2024 qualitative study of 17 parents trained in the approach, reporting experiences and not measured change. The approach's originator also founded the training company Studio 3, a conflict of interest for evaluations. Confidence: low. [Families' experiences of the Low Arousal Approach](https://pureportal.bcu.ac.uk/en/publications/families-experiences-of-the-low-arousal-approach-a-qualitative-st/); [Frontiers in Psychology 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1328825/pdf)
- The PDA Society describes low-arousal approaches as well evidenced, but cites the originator's own work and no independent trials. Treat as advocacy guidance. Confidence: low. [PDA Society](https://www.pdasociety.org.uk/what-helps-guides/pda-approaches/low-arousal-approaches/)

Self-advocacy, energy accounting, reduced demands
- Raymaker et al. 2020 (interviews plus public online posts by autistic adults): autistic burnout results from chronic life stress and a mismatch between expectations and abilities without adequate support. Participants said acceptance and social support, time off and reduced expectations, and doing things in an autistic way (unmasking) helped recovery. Confidence: low to medium (qualitative). [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/)
- Energy accounting is recommended in National Autistic Society guidance, but no research evaluating it was found. Confidence: low. [NAS: Understanding autistic burnout](https://autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/professional-practice/autistic-burnout)

Period preparation
- See section 4 (Steward et al. 2018 and NAS guidance).

### Inferences
- A sensible evidence tier for the app, from strongest to weakest: (A) visual supports, parent-delivered routines and sleep education, AAC, exercise or movement, antecedent trigger reduction; (B) choice-making, social narratives, ear defenders, transition warnings; (C) low-arousal approach, quiet space, decompression time, snack or drink, energy accounting, period preparation (plausible, widely recommended, not trialled); (D) weighted blankets for sleep and weighted vests (tested, no benefit shown).
- Because NCAEP stops at age 22 and most studies are in ages 3 to 11, any ranking for the adult band will depend much more on family logs than on published evidence. The app's line "Based on family logs, not a clinical trial" fits this.
- Tier C strategies are low-risk and need-focused, so "limited research, commonly reported as helpful" is a fairer label than "no evidence".

### Gaps
- The NCAEP age-by-outcome matrix (which practice has evidence for which outcome in bands 0 to 2, 3 to 5, 6 to 11, 12 to 14, 15 to 18, 19 to 22) could not be read: the NCAEP host was unreachable. The report PDF is at https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/EBP-Report-2020.pdf.
- NCAEP study counts for antecedent-based interventions, social narratives, exercise, AAC, and parent-implemented intervention were not retrieved.
- National Standards Project (National Autism Center) Phase 2 ratings, the AAP 2020 clinical report (Hyman et al.), NICE CG142 (adults), Autistica, and the Autism Speaks ATN toolkits were not retrieved in this session. No claims from them are made here.
- No study was found on offering a snack or drink, on a quiet space at home, or on fidget tools as stand-alone strategies for distress. These rest on the general guideline principle of checking physical needs and the environment as triggers.
- Exact wording of NICE CG170 recommendations 1.4.1 to 1.4.3 and the section 1.6 "do not use" list was not verified.

## 2. Where evidence is weak or negative

### Takeaway
Weighted blankets did not improve sleep in a well-run RCT, weighted vests are not evidence-based across four reviews, and sensory diets lack sufficient evidence. Social stories and visual schedules for transitions have weaker trial-level support than their popularity suggests, and information leaflets alone do not change sleep.

### Cited Findings
- Gringras et al. 2014 (Pediatrics 134(2):298 to 306): randomised, placebo-controlled crossover trial; 73 autistic children randomised, 67 analysed; two weeks with a weighted blanket and two weeks with an identical-looking usual-weight blanket. The weighted blanket did not lengthen total sleep, shorten time to fall asleep, or reduce night waking (actigraphy and parent diaries). Children and parents nonetheless preferred it and it was well tolerated. Confidence: high. [Gringras et al. 2014, Lancaster eprints](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/76682); [Guy's and St Thomas' summary](https://guysandstthomas.nhs.uk/news/heavy-blankets-do-not-help-children-autism-sleep)
- Safety context: a commentary notes the 2008 death of an autistic boy in Quebec while a weighted blanket was in use. Confidence: low (secondary source), but relevant to never covering the head or limiting a child's ability to remove the blanket. [Altogether Autism](https://www.altogetherautism.org.nz/do-weighted-blankets-improve-sleep-for-children-with-autism/)
- Taylor et al. 2017 systematic review: weighted vests cannot be considered an evidence-based practice for autistic individuals; too few studies show benefit and only two research teams have studied them. Confidence: high. [Taylor et al. 2017](https://brcasd.binghamton.edu/?p=290)
- Stephenson and Carter 2009 review: research on weighted vests is thin, they are not recommended, and heavy vests carry a possible injury risk. Confidence: medium. [ASAT synopsis of Stephenson and Carter](https://asatonline.org/research-treatment/research-synopses/the-use-and-the-efficacy-of-weighted-vests/)
- Lang et al. 2012 systematic review of sensory integration therapy (including weighted vests, swinging, brushing, joint compression): no consistently positive effect; evidence insufficient. Confidence: medium to high. [Lang et al. 2012, ASHA evidence map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/718008d6-315d-47e8-8855-4c178451240b)
- A 2025 systematic review of sensory-based interventions found limited evidence for weighted vests on attention and in-seat outcomes and distinguishes these from one-to-one Ayres Sensory Integration. Confidence: medium. [Frontiers in Pediatrics 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fped.2025.1720179)
- NCAEP companion paper: sensory diets and other sensory-based practices do not meet evidence-based criteria. Confidence: medium. [NCAEP sensory integration companion](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/sites/ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/files/resources/Sensory%20Integration_A%20Companion%20to%20the%20NCAEP%20Report.pdf)
- Social stories: the largest RCT (ASSSIST-2, 249 children) found no significant effect on the primary outcome. Confidence: high. [ASSSIST-2](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/216811/)
- Visual schedules for transitions: Mouzakes and Thompson 2025 found insufficient evidence that the schedule itself drives improvement; Scalzo et al. 2017 (three boys) found activity schedules alone were not effective when distress was about losing access to a preferred item. Confidence: medium. [Mouzakes and Thompson 2025](https://digitalcommons.wne.edu/casfacpubs/10/); [Activity schedules study, AASEP](https://www.aasep.org/article/view/349)
- A secondary summary of a 2026 meta-analysis (Adriaanse et al., 19 experiments) reports that warning signals alone did not, on average, significantly reduce transition distress. Confidence: low (blog summary with inconsistent attribution; verify before use). [Secondary summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/interventions-for-transition-related-challenging-behavior-in-individua/)
- Sleep education leaflet alone: no improvement in sleep latency (Adkins et al. 2012, n = 36). Confidence: medium. [Vanderbilt news](https://news.vumc.org/2012/11/01/studies-explore-best-ways-to-manage-insomnia-in-autism/)
- Low-arousal approach: no controlled outcome studies found. Confidence in this absence: medium. [Families' experiences of the Low Arousal Approach](https://pureportal.bcu.ac.uk/en/publications/families-experiences-of-the-low-arousal-approach-a-qualitative-st/)

### Inferences
- Weighted blankets are best shown as "liked by many families, did not improve sleep in a trial". A liking score and a sleep outcome score should be modelled separately in synthetic data: high acceptability, near-zero sleep effect.
- Weighted vests should not appear as a recommended strategy. Deep pressure in general (for example a firm hug the child asks for) has no trial evidence either way in these results and should be labelled as preference-based.
- Transition warnings and timers should be presented as "helps some children, raises anxiety in others", which argues for per-child tracking over a universal ranking.
- Leaflet-level information is not enough; the app's value is in helping caregivers apply a routine consistently, which is what the effective sleep education did.

### Gaps
- No trial was found on weighted blankets for daytime calming (as opposed to sleep) in autistic children.
- No evidence review on fidget tools for autistic children was retrieved.
- National Standards Project "unestablished" list was not retrieved.

## 3. Which strategies suit which age band

### Takeaway
Published evidence is concentrated in ages 2 to 11 and thins sharply after 14; adult evidence is mostly qualitative. Fit by age is therefore partly evidence and partly developmental reasoning: adult-led visual and routine supports for preschoolers, shared and increasingly self-managed supports for school age, collaborative and autonomy-respecting supports for teens, and self-directed energy management for adults.

### Cited Findings
- NCAEP 2020 covers ages 0 to 22 only, and summaries group visual-support evidence into 0 to 5, 6 to 14, and 15 to 22, with some outcomes supported only in certain bands. Confidence: medium. [Texas Autism Resource Guide](https://www.txautism.net/interventions/visual-supports)
- Ages 2 to 5: parent-mediated communication and interaction approaches were trialled in young children (Cochrane review of 17 RCTs in young autistic children; PACT enrolled preschoolers and followed them to a mean age of about 10.5). Confidence: high. [Oono et al. 2013 Cochrane](https://research.ncl.ac.uk/cargo-ne/CD009774.pdf); [NIHR Evidence on PACT follow-up](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/?p=38501)
- Ages 2 to 10: parent sleep education improved sleep latency (Malow et al. 2014, n = 80). Confidence: medium. [Malow et al. 2014](https://emmes.com/publications/14139)
- Ages 6 to 11: visual supports for transitions (Dettmer 2000, two elementary-age boys) and social stories (ASSSIST-2, primary schools) were studied in this band. Confidence: medium. [Dettmer et al. 2000](https://scilit.net/publications/29c92521d8c772b7c8ed4d89d3db5066); [ASSSIST-2](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/216811/)
- Ages 8 to 16: noise-attenuating headphones lowered physiological arousal (n = 6). Confidence: low to medium. [Pfeiffer et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31798424/)
- Exercise: age did not moderate the effect in a 22-study meta-analysis of autistic children, so movement appears usable across childhood. Confidence: medium. [Teh et al. 2022](https://mijn.bsl.nl/effects-of-physical-exercise-interventions-on-stereotyped-motor-/19346472)
- Adolescents: internalising symptoms rise more steeply in autistic girls through adolescence (UK Millennium Cohort analysis, 2025), and a 2025 qualitative study of ten autistic adolescents found camouflaging strategies harmed self-understanding and wellbeing. Confidence: medium (cohort), low (qualitative). [UCL Discovery 10217370](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10217370); [Autism 2025 qualitative study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/13623613251345855)
- Adults: recovery from burnout was linked by autistic adults to acceptance and support, time off and reduced expectations, and unmasking (Raymaker 2020). Vigorous exercise reduced distress behaviours in six autistic adults with intellectual disability (Elliott 1994). Confidence: low to medium. [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/); [Summary of Elliott et al. 1994](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/vigorous--aerobic-exercise-versus-general-motor-training-activities--e/)

### Inferences
Suggested strategy fit by band for app segmentation (reasoned from the findings above plus developmental logic; not a tested matrix):

| Strategy | 2 to 5 | 6 to 11 | 12 to 17 | Adult |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Visual schedule, first-then board | Strong fit, adult-led, objects or photos | Strong fit, child helps build it | Fit if self-managed (phone calendar, checklist) | Fit if self-chosen (planner, app) |
| Transition warning or timer | Fit, short warnings; watch for countdown anxiety | Fit | Fit when agreed with the teen, not imposed | Self-set reminders |
| Ear defenders or headphones | Fit if tolerated | Fit | Strong fit, teen controls use | Strong fit, self-directed |
| Quiet or low-arousal space | Fit, adult offers | Fit, child can request | Strong fit, private and unquestioned | Strong fit |
| Movement or break | Strong fit (play, outdoor time) | Strong fit | Fit, teen chooses activity | Fit, self-chosen |
| Social narrative, preparing for change | Simple picture stories | Best-studied band, mixed results | Replace with direct, honest advance information | Written plans, advance notice |
| Snack or drink check | Strong fit (cannot report hunger or thirst) | Fit | Self-serve access | Self-managed |
| Bedtime routine | Best-studied band | Studied to age 10 | Collaborative; later body clock in puberty needs negotiation | Self-managed sleep habits |
| Decompression after school or work | Less relevant before school | Strong fit | Strong fit | Strong fit (after work) |
| Choice-making | Two concrete options | Wider choices | Shift from offered choices to shared decisions | Full autonomy; supporter asks, does not decide |
| AAC at home | Start early, model without pressure | Continue | Continue, respect the person's preferred mode | Continue |
| Low-arousal approach by caregiver | Fit | Fit | Strong fit | Strong fit |
| Period preparation | Not applicable | Start before first period (roughly ages 8 to 11) | Ongoing practical support | Self-managed, see section 4 |
| Self-advocacy, energy accounting | Not applicable | Early version: naming feelings and needs | Strong fit | Strong fit |

- For teens the key change is who owns the support. The same tool (schedule, timer, headphones) can shift from adult-directed to self-directed. The app should let 12 to 17 logs record whether the young person chose or agreed the strategy, since imposed supports are likely to work worse.
- The "adult" band will have little published evidence behind any ranking, so the community-statistics threshold (5 or more families) matters most here.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head study comparing the same strategy across age bands was found.
- No controlled evidence was found on collaborative or autonomy-supportive approaches for autistic teens at home; the adolescent column rests on qualitative work and developmental reasoning.
- NICE CG142 (adults) was not retrieved, so adult guideline content is missing.
- No direct evidence was found on after-school decompression time as a strategy at any age.

## 4. Differences by sex or gender, masking-related exhaustion, and menstruation

### Takeaway
There is no trial evidence that any home strategy works differently by sex. What is documented is a difference in needs: autistic girls and women more often camouflage, show steeper rises in internalising distress in adolescence, and report that periods intensify sensory and emotional regulation difficulties. That supports adding need-based strategies (decompression, period preparation) and not sex-specific effectiveness scores.

### Cited Findings
- Cook, Hull, Crane, and Mandy 2021 systematic review: 29 studies of camouflaging in autistic children and adults; of 18 studies examining sex or gender, 12 suggested females camouflage more, across more contexts, more often, and for longer. Higher self-reported camouflaging was associated with worse mental health. The research base was limited in participant characterisation and representativeness. Confidence: medium. [Cook et al. 2021](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/camouflaging-in-autism-a-systematic-review/)
- A later systematic review and meta-analysis on male bias in autism diagnosis includes a meta-analysis of gender differences in camouflaging from ten studies (effect sizes not retrieved). Confidence: low for specifics. [PubMed 38285291](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38285291/)
- Raymaker et al. 2020: autistic adults described masking as exhausting and a route to burnout, while unmasking could bring ostracism and bullying; the authors caution against teaching autistic people to mask. Confidence: low to medium (qualitative). [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/)
- UK Millennium Cohort analysis (2025): female sex was linked to steeper increases in internalising symptoms among autistic young people; group differences from non-autistic peers appeared at about age 7 in males and age 11 in females. Confidence: medium. [UCL Discovery 10217370](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10217370)
- A 2024 study of 89 school-age autistic children (17 girls) found girls had more reported bullying and less anger control and emotional self-control than boys. Small female sample. Confidence: low. [JADD 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-024-06716-5)
- A 2019 pilot (84 adolescents, conference poster): autistic females reported the highest camouflaging and anxiety; autistic males reported the highest depression and stress. Confidence: low. [INSAR 2019](https://insar.confex.com/insar/2019/webprogram/Paper31256.html)
- Practice guidance: girls often mask at school and feel safe to show distress only at home, leading to distress after school. This is guidance, not research. Confidence: low. [BeyondAutism](https://www.beyondautism.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/BeyondAutism_Autistic_Women_Girls.pdf)
- Steward et al. 2018 (JADD 48(12):4287 to 4292): online survey of post-menarche autistic (n = 123) and non-autistic (n = 114) respondents. Autistic respondents reported that periods amplified sensory differences and difficulties regulating emotion and behaviour, with significant negative impact on daily life. Respondents wanted to have been told plainly what happens before the first period so it is not a shock, and found existing information too vague or inaccurate. Sample counts vary slightly between versions of the paper. Confidence: medium (preliminary survey). [Steward et al. 2018, UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050187/)
- National Autistic Society professional guidance summarises this work and practical preparation. Confidence: low to medium (guidance). [NAS: Autistic people and menstruation](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/professional-practice/menstruation)
- Most intervention research has male-majority samples; for example the transition study by Dettmer (two boys) and the activity schedule study by Scalzo (three boys). Radesky's general-population study found the link between device-based calming and later emotional dysregulation was stronger in boys. Confidence: medium. [Dettmer et al. 2000](https://scilit.net/publications/29c92521d8c772b7c8ed4d89d3db5066); [AAP Q and A](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/autism-media-and-emotion-regulation/)

### Inferences
- Segmenting strategy rankings by sex is not supported by effectiveness evidence. A defensible design is to rank by age band and trigger, and to surface a small set of need-specific strategies when relevant: period preparation and cycle-aware logging for people who menstruate, and decompression or reduced demands after masking-heavy settings for anyone, with a note that this pattern is reported more often in girls and women.
- A useful app feature is an optional cycle tag on logs, since autistic respondents report distress and sensory sensitivity rising around periods. The app must present this as a "possible thing to check", not a cause.
- Period preparation principles from the survey: start before the first period, use concrete and literal information, show the actual products, practise the steps, plan for sensory preferences in products, and keep a visual checklist. These are inferences from respondents' stated wishes, not tested protocols.
- Gender-diverse autistic people are barely represented in these sources; the app should key period supports to "people who menstruate" and not to a sex field.

### Gaps
- No study was found testing whether any listed strategy has different effects in girls versus boys or women versus men.
- No intervention study was found on period preparation for autistic girls; evidence is survey and guidance only.
- No quantified sex difference in after-school distress was found; this pattern is clinical description.
- The camouflaging meta-analysis effect size was not retrieved.
- Nothing was retrieved on non-binary or transgender autistic people and strategy fit.

## 5. Parent-mediated approaches doable at home (principles only)

### Takeaway
Parent-delivered approaches have RCT and Cochrane-level support for improving parent and child interaction and, modestly, autism-related outcomes in young children, and parent sleep education improves sleep onset. The transferable principles are responsiveness, following the child's lead, predictable routines, and adjusting the environment, not teaching the child to perform.

### Cited Findings
- Oono et al. 2013 Cochrane review: 17 RCTs of parent-mediated early intervention in young autistic children. Clearest gains were in parent and child interaction; some evidence for language comprehension and for reduced autism severity (SMD -0.30, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.08). Most other primary outcomes were not statistically clear, parent stress findings were inconclusive, and most studies had high risk of bias. Confidence: medium to high. [Oono et al. 2013](https://research.ncl.ac.uk/cargo-ne/CD009774.pdf)
- PACT (Pickles et al. 2016, Lancet): 152 children in the original trial; 121 (80%) followed up at mean age about 10.5. Group difference in autism symptom severity at treatment end (effect size 0.64, 95% CI 0.07 to 1.20); at follow-up the estimate was 0.70 but the interval crossed zero (-0.05 to 1.47). Language composite showed no group difference (0.15), and adaptive and prosocial outcomes were not significantly better. Later descriptions of "sustained" benefit rest on the parent and child communication measure and mediation analysis. Confidence: high for the numbers, medium for long-term benefit. [NIHR Evidence](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/?p=38501); [PACT follow-up full text](https://core.ac.uk/download/74193573.pdf)
- PACT is a clinician-coached programme; only its principles (parents noticing and responding to the child's own communication) are in scope for an app. Confidence: high. [PACT training research summary](https://www.pacttraining.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Research-Outreach-2020.pdf)
- Parent sleep education (Malow et al. 2014, 80 children aged 2 to 10): improved sleep latency and family functioning in a few sessions, with group delivery as good as individual. A leaflet alone did not work. Confidence: medium. [Malow et al. 2014](https://emmes.com/publications/14139); [Vanderbilt news](https://news.vumc.org/2012/11/01/studies-explore-best-ways-to-manage-insomnia-in-autism/)
- NICE guidance directs supporters to look first for triggers (physical health, mental health, environment) when distress behaviour appears. Confidence: high. [NICE QS51 statement 7](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs51/chapter/Quality-statement-7-Assessing-possible-triggers-for-behaviour-that-challenges)
- Parents trained in the low-arousal approach described their experiences in a qualitative study of 17 parents; no measured outcomes. Confidence: low. [Frontiers in Psychology 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1328825/pdf)

### Inferences
Principles a caregiver can apply without a clinician, each consistent with the sources above:
1. Check needs and triggers first: pain, illness, hunger, thirst, tiredness, noise, change in routine. (Red flags route to "Please contact your doctor or care team".)
2. Follow the child's lead and respond to every communication attempt, in whatever form.
3. Make the day predictable and show it visually; give advance notice of change.
4. Lower demands and sensory load when the child is already stretched.
5. Offer real choices.
6. Keep a consistent, calm wind-down before bed with a regular bedtime and wake time.
7. Stay calm, reduce talking, and give space during high distress.
8. Build movement and breaks into the day.
- The sleep findings imply that consistent application, not information alone, drives benefit. Logging plus reminders is a reasonable app role.

### Gaps
- NCAEP's Parent-Implemented Intervention study counts and age bands were not retrieved.
- No parent-mediated trial data were found for adolescents or adults.
- The specific content of the Malow sleep curriculum (for example exact routine steps) was not retrieved; the Autism Speaks ATN sleep toolkit was not accessed.

## 6. Approaches families and autistic people report as unhelpful or harmful ("not recommended")

### Takeaway
The best-documented cautions are using screens as the main calming tool (associated with worse emotional regulation over time in young children generally), pressure to make eye contact (described as aversive by autistic people), punishment, and teaching masking. Evidence is mostly observational or qualitative, so the app should say "not recommended" with reasons, not claim proven harm.

### Cited Findings
- Radesky et al. (JAMA Pediatrics; 422 parents and children aged 3 to 5, general population, not autism-specific): frequent use of devices to calm a child was associated with more emotional dysregulation later, particularly in boys and in children already struggling with regulation. Correlational. Confidence: medium. [AAP Q and A on autism, media, and emotion regulation](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/autism-media-and-emotion-regulation/); [news coverage](https://wishtv.com/?p=864696)
- AAP notes that parents of autistic children report using screens more to keep their child calm during stressful times, and suggests keeping media low (perhaps under 1 hour a day) for children with social and emotional difficulties. Confidence: medium (expert guidance). [AAP Q and A](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/autism-media-and-emotion-regulation/)
- A 2024 systematic review found autistic people tend to have longer daily screen use and higher risk of problematic use than non-autistic people. Confidence: medium. [PubMed 39627647](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39627647/)
- Ending screen time is itself a common transition trigger; guidance suggests aligning the end with a natural stopping point, and notes countdowns help some children and upset others. Confidence: low (guidance). [Autism New Zealand](https://autismnz.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Screen-time-1.pdf)
- Eye contact: Trevisan et al. 2017 (PLoS ONE; qualitative analysis of first-hand accounts from autistic teens and adults, self-declared diagnosis) found adverse emotional and physiological reactions, feelings of being invaded, and sensory overload during eye contact. Confidence: medium (qualitative). [Trevisan et al. 2017](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/19017)
- Compliance-focused behavioural programmes: Kupferstein 2018 (online survey, 460 autistic adults and caregivers) reported 46% of those exposed met a PTSD symptom threshold. The study has been strongly criticised (Leaf et al.) for design bias, a vague definition of exposure, and unconfirmed diagnoses. McGill and Robinson 2020 (qualitative interviews with autistic adults) reported predominantly detrimental experiences. Confidence: low; treat as a signal of reported harm, not a prevalence estimate. [Kupferstein 2018](https://emeraldinsight.com/doi/abs/10.1108/AIA-08-2017-0016); [McGill and Robinson 2020](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/AIA-02-2019-0004/full/html)
- Masking: Raymaker et al. 2020 caution against teaching autistic people to mask, given its link with exhaustion and burnout. Confidence: low to medium. [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/)
- Physical punishment: Human Rights Watch 2009 reported US students with disabilities made up 19% of those receiving corporal punishment but 14% of students, and documented physical and psychological injury. Policy report, not autism-specific outcome research. Confidence: low to medium. [Human Rights Watch 2009](https://www.hrw.org/reports/2009/08/11/impairing-education-0)
- NICE CG170 has a dedicated section (1.6) on interventions that should not be used for autism. Contents not verified here. Confidence: high that the section exists. [NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170)
- Weighted vests carry a possible injury risk and no demonstrated benefit. Confidence: medium. [ASAT synopsis](https://asatonline.org/research-treatment/research-synopses/the-use-and-the-efficacy-of-weighted-vests/)

### Inferences
- Realistic "not recommended" entries for synthetic data: screen as the main calming tool (short-term calm, later distress when the screen ends; show as low or negative net score), insisting on eye contact, punishment or removing comfort items, raising voice or adding demands during distress, pushing through a meltdown, taking away stimming, withdrawing breaks, and weighted vests. These should show poor outcomes without implying clinical findings.
- Screen use is not uniformly harmful: the evidence concerns screens as the routine response to distress. Suggested wording: "Screens may calm in the moment, but families often report harder transitions afterwards. A possible thing to check is how the screen time ends."
- A two-phase outcome for screens (calm within 10 minutes, new distress episode at screen removal) would make synthetic data plausible and consistent with the correlational evidence.

### Gaps
- No autism-specific longitudinal study was found linking screen-based calming to later transition distress. The link is extrapolated from a general-population preschool cohort plus guidance.
- No outcome study was found on "forced eye contact" as a taught target; the evidence is autistic people's accounts of eye contact itself.
- No longitudinal study was found on punishment and outcomes specifically in autistic children.
- No survey ranking the strategies families themselves rate as least helpful was found.

## 7. Real-world outcome sizes for plausible synthetic numbers

### Takeaway
Published effect sizes are small to moderate and highly variable between individuals, and several popular strategies show null results in trials. No source gave a clean "percentage reduction in transition distress with visual schedules", so synthetic numbers should be modest (roughly 25 to 55% of logged uses rated helpful for most strategies) with wide spread between families.

### Cited Findings
- Exercise on stereotyped behaviours: g = 1.16 under a liberal model, g = 0.51 under a conservative one (22 studies, 274 children); d about -0.46 in separate small-n meta-analyses. Confidence: medium to high. [Teh et al. 2022](https://mijn.bsl.nl/effects-of-physical-exercise-interventions-on-stereotyped-motor-/19346472); [Tarr et al. 2020](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100097028954_0x000001)
- Parent-mediated early intervention on autism severity: SMD -0.30 (95% CI -0.52 to -0.08). Confidence: medium to high. [Oono et al. 2013](https://research.ncl.ac.uk/cargo-ne/CD009774.pdf)
- PACT: effect size 0.64 at treatment end; 0.70 at follow-up with an interval crossing zero. Confidence: high. [NIHR Evidence](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/?p=38501)
- Social stories: about 51% of single-case studies showed benefit (secondary report of Kokina and Kern 2010); RCT difference of -1.61 points on SRS-2, not significant; -3.37 points with at least six sessions. Confidence: medium. [Kokina and Kern 2010, ASHA evidence map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/b8a776ce-b372-40f9-8f48-7659cbe61c75); [ASSSIST-2](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/216811/)
- Weighted blankets: no difference in total sleep time, sleep onset, or night waking, yet preferred by children and parents (67 analysed). Confidence: high. [Gringras et al. 2014](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/76682)
- Activity schedules: reduced distress behaviour in all 18 reviewed single-case studies, but alone were ineffective for three boys whose distress related to losing a preferred item. Confidence: medium. [Lequia et al. 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ947663); [AASEP activity schedule study](https://www.aasep.org/article/view/349)
- Choice-making: "clinically significant" reductions across 13 studies and 30 participants; no percentage retrieved. Confidence: medium. [Shogren et al. 2004](https://vufind.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c339t-58c543743509d93658701567e03650c4dd5bed494745628a448050c8cc5a48583)
- Sleep education: entry criterion in the related trial was sleep latency of 30 minutes or more; education improved latency, a leaflet did not. Minutes gained were not retrieved. Confidence: medium. [Vanderbilt news](https://news.vumc.org/2012/11/01/studies-explore-best-ways-to-manage-insomnia-in-autism/)
- Headphones: significant fall in skin conductance in 6 children; no behavioural percentage reported. Confidence: low to medium. [Pfeiffer et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31798424/)

### Inferences
These are modelling suggestions for synthetic data, not published figures:
- Single-case studies (which dominate this literature) report larger effects than group trials, and published single-case work over-represents successes. Family-log "helped" rates should therefore sit well below the near-universal success implied by reviews such as Lequia 2012.
- Plausible per-use "helped" rates by tier: tier A strategies (visual schedule, movement break, bedtime routine, trigger reduction) 45 to 60%; tier B (choice, transition warning, ear defenders, social narrative) 35 to 50%; tier C (quiet space, decompression, snack or drink, low-arousal response) 35 to 55% with wider variance and strong dependence on trigger match; weighted blanket for sleep 15 to 25% on sleep outcome despite 60 to 70% "child likes it"; weighted vest 10 to 20%; screen as calming 60 to 70% immediate calm paired with 30 to 40% follow-on distress at screen removal; punishment or raised voice 5 to 10% with frequent escalation.
- A standardised effect of about 0.3 to 0.5 corresponds roughly to a 15 to 25 percentage-point shift in the share of episodes that settle, which is a realistic ceiling for group-level differences between a good-fit and an average strategy.
- Trigger match should matter more than age in the synthetic model: ear defenders should score high for noise triggers and near baseline for hunger; snack or drink the reverse; visual schedules and warnings high for transition and change triggers and weak for tangible-loss triggers (consistent with Scalzo 2017).
- Age interaction to encode: adult-directed visual supports and social narratives decline in helpfulness from 6 to 11 into 12 to 17 unless marked as chosen by the young person; self-directed tools (headphones, quiet space, decompression, energy planning) rise with age.
- Sex should not change base effectiveness in the synthetic data. If modelled at all, vary trigger frequency (more after-school and masking-related episodes and cycle-linked episodes for girls and women), not strategy effect.
- With the "5 or more families" display rule, small cells (for example adult women using period supports) will often be hidden; that is realistic and consistent with how thin the evidence is.

### Gaps
- No source gave a percentage reduction in transition-related distress with visual schedules or timers. Dettmer 2000 reports shorter transition latency for two boys, but the figures were not retrieved.
- Minutes of sleep latency change in Malow 2014 were not retrieved (full text unreachable).
- No autism-specific pooled effect size for choice-making, AAC on distress behaviour, or antecedent-based interventions was retrieved.
- No real-world family-reported helpfulness rates (for example large caregiver surveys ranking strategies) were found; the per-use rates above are inference only and should be labelled synthetic.
