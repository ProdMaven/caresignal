# Distress presentations and triggers in autistic people by age band

Scope note: these notes support realistic synthetic data for a caregiver logging app. They are not diagnostic. Medication, restraint, and clinician-supervised interventions are excluded.

Verification note (applies to every finding below): pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov were blocked in this research session, so no primary full text could be opened. Figures come from abstracts, press releases, and summaries surfaced by web search. Each finding is tagged:
- [V] = figure or statement appeared directly in search results this session (abstract-level or reputable summary).
- [R] = from researcher recall of the paper, not re-checked this session. Treat as lower confidence until checked.
Confidence (high, medium, low) reflects both study strength and how well the figure was verified.

Age bands: toddler and preschool (2 to 5), school age (6 to 11), adolescent (12 to 17), adult (18+).

## 1. Which distress presentations are most common in each age band, with prevalence numbers?

### Takeaway
Aggression toward others and tantrum or meltdown style distress are most common in the youngest children and tend to ease with age, while withdrawal and shutdown style presentations become relatively more prominent in adolescence, and adults mainly describe internally experienced meltdowns, shutdowns, and burnout. Prevalence numbers vary widely by definition (about 25% on a validated clinical cutoff, about 53% for "current concern", 68% for "ever toward a caregiver"), and no study gives clean per-band rates for meltdown, shutdown, food refusal, or stimming increases.

### Cited Findings

Aggression toward others (all child bands)
- Kanne and Mazurek 2011: 1,380 autistic children and adolescents aged 4 to 17 (Simons Simplex Collection, parent report). 68% had ever shown aggression toward a caregiver and 49% toward non-caregivers. Later papers cite the "current" figures from the same study as 56% toward caregivers and 32% toward non-caregivers. Sample: large cross-sectional research cohort, simplex families (may skew toward higher IQ). Confidence: high for the figures [V], medium for generalizability. Source: [Kennedy Krieger IAN summary](https://www.kennedykrieger.org/stories/interactive-autism-network-ian/aggression_and_asd); [Springer record of the paper](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10803-010-1118-4); [Hill et al. 2014 discussion of prior estimates](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4160737)
- Kanne and Mazurek 2011 found aggression associated with younger age. Confidence: medium [V, via summary and review table]. They also reported that sex, language level, and IQ were not strong predictors while repetitive behaviors and self-injury were [R]. Confidence: low until rechecked. Source: [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10803-010-1118-4)
- Mazurek, Kanne, and Wodka 2013: 1,584 autistic children and adolescents (Autism Treatment Network registry, single yes or no parent item on whether physical aggression is a current concern). Prevalence 53% (53.7% in later citations), "highest in young children". Aggression was associated with self-injury, sleep problems, sensory problems, GI problems, and communication and social functioning. Sample: large clinical registry, cross-sectional. Confidence: high for headline figure [V]; per-age-band rates not retrieved. Source: [SPARK summary](https://sparkforautism.org/discover_article/understanding-aggressive-behavior-in-autism/); [Disability Scoop coverage](https://www.disabilityscoop.com/?p=17510); [Hill et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4160737)
- Hill et al. 2014: about 1 in 4 autistic children scored in the clinical range (T score 70 or above) on the Child Behavior Checklist Aggressive Behavior scale. The authors note that studies using validated measures report lower prevalence than single-item parent reports. A linked conference abstract from the same site describes 400 children, 83% male, mean age 5.4, range 2.0 to 16.9. Sample: ATN clinical sample, cross-sectional. Confidence: high for the 25% figure [V]; medium that the published sample matches the abstract. Source: [Hill et al. 2014, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4160737); [BibBase record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/hill-zuckerman-hagen-kriz-duvall-vansanten-nigg-fair-etal-aggressivebehaviorproblemsinchildrenwithautismspectrumdisordersprevalenceandcorrelatesinalargeclinicalsample-2014); [IMFAR 2013 abstract](https://imfar.confex.com/imfar/2013/webprogram/Paper14863.html)

Tantrums and meltdown style distress (2 to 5)
- Mayes, Lockridge, and Tierney 2017: 240 autistic children aged 15 to 71 months. IQ, language comprehension, and speech clarity together explained less than 3% of the variance in tantrum frequency. Children with speech at roughly a 2 year old level had more tantrums than children with less speech. The authors point to mood dysregulation and low frustration tolerance as more likely factors, to be studied further. Sample: clinical, cross-sectional, single site. Confidence: medium to high [V]. Source: [Penn State news](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/speech-and-language-deficits-children-autism-may-not-cause-tantrums); [Penn State Pure record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/tantrums-are-not-associated-with-speech-or-language-deficits-in-p/)
- A 2016 Duke conference abstract reported significant differences in the frequency and nature of tantrums between autistic preschoolers and a neurotypical community sample. Abstract only; numbers not retrieved. Confidence: low. Source: [IMFAR 2016 abstract](https://imfar.confex.com/imfar/2016/webprogram/Paper20937.html)
- Reference point from non-autistic children: in a community sample of 861 children aged 1 to 5, tantrum frequency declined with age while tantrum duration increased; frequent tantrums related to externalizing problems and long tantrums to internalizing problems. Not an autistic sample. Confidence: medium as a general developmental baseline only. Source: [University of Amsterdam repository](https://dare.uva.nl/id/0ddaf613-cce9-40a1-bfb1-e1b6da6f4dc1)
- In 26 autistic children aged 4 to 6, baseline negative affectivity (temperament) uniquely predicted later frequency of distress behavior. Small longitudinal sample. Confidence: low to medium. Source: [summary of temperament study](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/temperament-predicts-challenging-behavior-in-children-with-autism-spec/)

School age into adolescence (6 to 17): trajectories
- Anderson, Maye, and Lord 2011: children referred before 37 months and followed with the Aberrant Behavior Checklist from age 9 to 18. Hyperactivity improved the most across groups. Irritability improved less. Social withdrawal worsened with age for a substantial proportion of autistic youths but not for the non-spectrum comparison group. Autistic youths showed more varied trajectories than comparison youths. Sample: longitudinal clinic-referred cohort (a press summary describes 142 children). Confidence: high for direction, medium for exact proportions [V]. Source: [Anderson et al. 2011, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3185304); [The Transmitter, "Teenage wasteland"](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/teenage-wasteland/)
- A later longitudinal study (late childhood to early adulthood, parent and teacher report) found hyperactivity improved most, irritability improved less, and social withdrawal stayed stable. Confidence: medium [V, summary level]. Source: [eScholarship full text](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2608g6wz/qt2608g6wz.pdf); [medRxiv preprint on teacher and parent trajectories](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.09.09.22279781.full.pdf)
- Puberty and aggression: no study with a percentage of autistic adolescents whose distress worsens at puberty was found. Clinical commentary describes puberty-linked increases in some young people, based on case reports and practitioner experience; the same expert source notes that across adolescence and adulthood hyperactivity and irritability often decrease. Confidence: low (anecdotal). Source: [Autism Speaks expert opinion](https://autismspeaks.org/expert-opinion/autism-puberty-aggression); [The Transmitter on puberty](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/families-struggle-to-cope-with-autism-and-puberty/)

School distress (peaks in school age, persists in adolescence)
- Connolly, Constable, and Mullally 2023 (Frontiers in Psychiatry): online survey of 947 UK parents of children with school distress plus 149 age-matched controls. 94.3% of school attendance problems were underpinned by significant emotional distress. 83.4% of children currently experiencing school distress were autistic versus 16.8% in the no-distress group (odds ratio 46.61, 95% CI 24.67 to 88.07). Mean age of the children was 11.6; mean onset of school distress was 7.94 years in autistic children versus 8.78 in non-autistic children, and it lasted longer in autistic children. Sample: self-selected parent survey, limited diversity, so the 83.4% is not a population rate. Confidence: medium [V]. Source: [Frontiers in Psychiatry](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1237052/pdf); [Autism Research Institute summary](https://autism.org/school-distress-rates-are-elevated-among-those-with-autism/)

Adults
- Shattuck et al. 2007: 241 autistic adolescents and adults aged 10 to 52 followed about 4.5 years by parent interview. Most stayed stable; among those who changed, more improved than worsened in autism features and in distress behaviors. Adults aged 31 and older had fewer such behaviors and improved more than adolescents. Co-occurring intellectual disability was linked to more features and less improvement. No sex difference in degree of change. Confidence: high [V]. Source: [Washington University release](https://source.washu.edu/2007/09/study-shows-autism-symptoms-can-improve-into-adulthood/); [journal record](https://mijn.bsl.nl/change-in-autism-symptoms-and-maladaptive-behaviors-in-adolescen/546070)
- Lewis and Stevens 2023: written online interviews with 32 autistic adults in seven countries about meltdowns. Adults described being overwhelmed by sensory input, emotional stress, social situations, information overload, or unexpected change, often after stressors accumulated; losing access to logic and memory; and struggling for self-control. The experience is described as largely internal and painful, beyond what is visible. Many used isolation, safe spaces, trigger avoidance, masking, or early warning signs. Sample: qualitative, speaking or writing adults only. Confidence: medium [V]. Source: [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36632658/); [Aspect "Research unpacked: meltdowns"](https://www.aspect.org.au/resources/research-unpacked-meltdowns)
- Shutdowns in adults: a descriptive phenomenology study of 33 adults recruited through online forums treated shutdown as a distinct phenomenon from meltdown. Only a conference abstract was found. Confidence: low. Source: [University of Vermont abstract](https://scholarworks.uvm.edu/src/2019/program/91)
- Shutdown is described in guidance as triggered by the same things as a meltdown, but the person "freezes" or "switches off" rather than showing outward distress. Guidance, not prevalence data. Confidence: medium as description. Source: [Autistica](https://www.autistica.org.uk/what-is-autism/supporting-meltdowns); [NHS Scotland Right Decisions](https://rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/autism-meeting-the-needs-of-autistic-people/autistic-meltdownsshutdowns)

### Inferences
- For synthetic data, a defensible relative weighting (inference, not measured rates):
  - 2 to 5: meltdown or tantrum style events dominate; aggression toward caregivers common; transition distress and food refusal frequent; elopement plausible; shutdown rare in logs because caregivers of young children rarely label it.
  - 6 to 11: meltdowns remain common but shift toward home and after school; school-related distress appears (mean onset near age 8 in the school distress survey); aggression somewhat lower than preschool; increased stimming logged around school demands.
  - 12 to 17: fewer outward meltdowns, more shutdown, withdrawal, and exhaustion; irritability lower on average than in childhood but with high person-to-person variation; a subset shows more intense events around puberty (anecdotal).
  - Adult: lowest rate of outward events on average; shutdown, withdrawal, loss of speech in the moment, and burnout periods; outward distress persists most where intellectual disability co-occurs.
- A realistic aggression flag rate depends on definition. If the synthetic persona logs "any hitting, kicking, biting toward others", a "current" rate near half of children fits Mazurek 2013; "clinically significant" should be nearer one quarter (Hill 2014).
- High person-to-person variance should be built in: Anderson 2011 specifically found more heterogeneous trajectories in autistic youths.

### Gaps
- No per-band prevalence found for meltdown frequency, shutdown, increased stimming, food refusal, or transition distress as logged event types. These terms are caregiver and community language and rarely appear as measured outcomes.
- Per-age-group aggression percentages from Mazurek 2013 and Hill 2014 were not retrieved (full texts blocked). Only "highest in young children" is confirmed.
- No adult prevalence figures for meltdown or shutdown frequency were found; adult evidence is qualitative.
- The Duke 2016 tantrum comparison figures were not retrievable.

## 2. Which triggers or antecedent contexts are most reported in each age band?

### Takeaway
The same core triggers (sensory load, change or unpredictability, anxiety, and communication difficulty) are cited across all ages; what changes with age is the setting and the load: frustration and transitions in early childhood, school demands from about age 7 to 8, masking, social exhaustion, and withdrawal in adolescence, and cumulative overload leading to burnout in teens and adults. Trigger rankings by age band are not quantified in the literature; most age-specific trigger claims rest on guidance and qualitative work.

### Cited Findings

Across ages
- The National Autistic Society lists sensory differences, changes in routine, anxiety, and communication difficulties as common meltdown triggers, notes that every autistic person is different, and advises recording what happened before, during, and after, because meltdowns can cluster at particular times, places, or after particular events. Guidance from a major charity. Confidence: medium (expert consensus, not quantified). Source: [National Autistic Society, Meltdowns](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/behaviour/meltdowns/all-audiences)
- Autistica lists sensory overload, social overwhelm, uncertainty, and unexpected changes. Confidence: medium. Source: [Autistica](https://www.autistica.org.uk/what-is-autism/supporting-meltdowns)
- The Autism Society (US) adds unclear communication or vague instructions, unpredictable delays (waiting), crowded spaces, and past negative experiences. Confidence: medium. Source: [Autism Society guide, 2025](https://autismsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/AutismSociety_Autistic-Meltdowns-Shutdowns_2025-06V2F_Digital.pdf)
- Raising Children Network (Australia) advises a 1 to 2 week diary noting time of day, sensory input, tiredness, hunger, and the activity just before (for example getting ready for school or switching tasks). This supports hunger, tiredness, and transitions as standard antecedent fields. Confidence: medium (guidance). Source: [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/behaviour/common-concerns/aggressive-behaviour-asd)
- Co-occurring conditions as background load: in 1,584 autistic children, aggression was associated with sleep problems, sensory problems, GI problems, and self-injury (cross-sectional association, not proof of cause). Confidence: high for association [V]. Source: [SPARK summary of Mazurek et al. 2013](https://sparkforautism.org/discover_article/understanding-aggressive-behavior-in-autism/)
- In 81 autistic children, sleep problems were associated with physical aggression, irritability, inattention, and hyperactivity. Small clinical sample. Confidence: medium. Source: [summary of Mazurek and Sohl sleep study](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/sleep-and-behavioral-problems-in-children-with-autism-spectrum-disorde/)

Toddler and preschool (2 to 5)
- Communication frustration is the most popular explanation for tantrums, but Mayes et al. 2017 (240 children, 15 to 71 months) found speech and language explained under 3% of tantrum variance; low frustration tolerance and mood regulation were proposed instead. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [Penn State news](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/speech-and-language-deficits-children-autism-may-not-cause-tantrums)
- Transitions: autistic children's reactions to transitions tend to be stronger than other children's because of a preference for routine and predictability; signs listed in practitioner sources include crying, refusing to move, dropping to the floor, and running away. Practitioner sources, not studies. Confidence: low to medium. Source: [Child Mind Institute](https://childmind.org/article/why-do-kids-have-trouble-with-transitions/); [Behavioral Directions](https://www.behavioraldirections.com/autism/helping-children-with-autism-handle-changes-in-routine-and-transitions/)

School age (6 to 11)
- School itself becomes a main context: in the Connolly 2023 survey, school distress began at a mean of 7.94 years in autistic children and was more enduring than in non-autistic peers. Confidence: medium. Source: [Frontiers in Psychiatry](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1237052/pdf)

Adolescent (12 to 17)
- Camouflaging at school: Halsall, Clarke, and Crane 2021 interviewed 8 autistic adolescent girls, their mothers, and educators. Exhaustion was identified as the most impactful consequence of camouflaging, and one girl described being more angry at home after hiding anger at school. Sample: very small, girls only, UK resource-base schools. Confidence: low to medium. Source: [Autism journal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13623613211012819)
- Bernardin et al. 2021, "You Must Become a Chameleon to Survive": questionnaires (n = 132) and interviews (n = 19) with autistic and non-autistic adolescents, including boys, on camouflaging experience. Specific findings not retrieved. Confidence: low for any detail. Source: [Illinois Experts record](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/you-must-become-a-chameleon-to-survive-adolescent-experiences-of-/)
- Two qualitative studies report autistic girls saying camouflaging caused high anxiety within school (as cited in a later JADD paper on school belonging, camouflaging, and anxiety). Confidence: low to medium. Source: [JADD, school belonging and camouflaging](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-024-06668-w)
- Social withdrawal worsening across ages 9 to 18 for a substantial proportion of autistic youths (Anderson 2011) is consistent with social demands outpacing capacity in adolescence, though the study did not test triggers. Confidence: medium for the trend. Source: [Anderson et al. 2011, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3185304)

Teens and adults: autistic burnout
- Raymaker et al. 2020 (Autism in Adulthood): interviews with 19 autistic adults plus analysis of public online posts. Burnout was characterized by chronic exhaustion, loss of skills (speech, executive function, memory, self-care), and reduced tolerance to stimulus, typically lasting 3 months or more. It was attributed to life stressors plus the cumulative load of masking, with expectations outweighing abilities and inadequate support. Described as distinct from occupational burnout and from depression. Effects included worse physical and mental health and reduced capacity for independent living. Sample: qualitative, small, adults; not a clinical term and no checklist exists. Confidence: medium (well regarded, but small and qualitative). Source: [PubMed record](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/); [Portland State repository](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/socwork_fac/378); [National Autistic Society, Understanding autistic burnout](https://autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/professional-practice/autistic-burnout)
- Adult meltdown triggers in Lewis and Stevens 2023: sensory input, emotional stress, social situations, information overload, unexpected changes, usually cumulative rather than a single event. Confidence: medium. Source: [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36632658/)

### Inferences
- Suggested trigger emphasis per band for synthetic data (inference from the above, not measured frequencies):
  - 2 to 5: transitions, denied requests, waiting, screen time ending, hunger, tiredness after poor sleep, sensory (noise, clothing texture). Communication difficulty can be present but should not be modeled as the sole driver.
  - 6 to 11: school demands, after-school fatigue, routine change (substitute teacher, cancelled activity), sensory load in crowds and noisy settings, screen time ending, poor sleep.
  - 12 to 17: cumulative social exhaustion and masking, academic pressure, poor sleep, sensory load, unexpected change; triggers are more often "accumulated day" than a single event.
  - Adult: cumulative overload (sensory plus social plus demands), unexpected change, information overload; burnout periods lasting weeks to months where tolerance for all triggers drops.
- "Reduced tolerance to stimulus" during burnout implies that in synthetic data the same sensory trigger should produce distress at lower intensity during a burnout period.
- Cumulative build-up means a realistic pattern is lagged: several loaded days, then an event after a minor trigger.
- Screen time ending, hunger, waiting, and denied requests appear only in guidance-level sources here; they are plausible and widely reported by caregivers but not quantified by age.

### Gaps
- No study was found ranking triggers by frequency within age bands.
- No peer-reviewed data found on screen time ending, hunger, waiting, or denied requests as antecedents specifically in autistic children by age.
- NICE CG170 and CG142 and the AAP 2020 clinical report were not reached this session (searches returned nothing usable), so their lists of factors to assess are not cited. [R] CG170 advises assessing factors such as communication difficulty, co-occurring physical conditions including pain, co-occurring mental health conditions, the physical and sensory environment, the social environment, and changes to routine or circumstances, and developmental change including puberty. This should be confirmed at https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170 before citing.
- Evidence on autistic burnout in under 18s is thin; Raymaker 2020 sampled adults only, though participants described onset around life transitions.
- Bernardin 2021 details not retrieved.

## 3. How do sensory sensitivities and their behavioral effects change with age?

### Takeaway
Sensory differences are present in most autistic people at every age and persist into adulthood; the best-known meta-analysis suggests they may rise through early childhood, peak around ages 6 to 9, and ease somewhat afterward, but that pattern comes from heterogeneous cross-sectional data and longitudinal studies mostly show stability. What changes more clearly with age is the response: younger children show outward distress, while adolescents and adults more often report avoidance, withdrawal, exhaustion, and, in burnout, lowered tolerance.

### Cited Findings
- Prevalence in children: unusual sensory reactivity reported in 69% (Baranek et al. 2006) to about 95% (Leekam et al. 2007; Tomchek and Dunn 2007) of autistic children, as summarized by Elwin et al. Confidence: high for the range [V via secondary citation]. Source: [Elwin et al., sensory clusters of adults](https://mijn.bsl.nl/sensory-clusters-of-adults-with-and-without-autism-spectrum-cond/11236216)
- Questionnaire studies suggest atypical sensory perception in over 90% of autistic individuals (stated in Tavassoli, Hoekstra, and Baron-Cohen 2014, Molecular Autism). Confidence: medium (summary statement of prior studies). Source: [PMC record surfaced in search](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5352790); [Open University repository](https://oro.open.ac.uk/40103)
- Age pattern: the Ben-Sasson et al. 2009 meta-analysis of 14 cross-sectional studies is usually cited as showing a non-linear trend, with some sensory features increasing up to about ages 6 to 9 and decreasing after age 9. Critiques note heterogeneity (participant ages from 7 months to 56 years). A later update covered 55 studies; its age results were not confirmed. Confidence: medium to low for the peak. Source: summaries surfaced in search at [PMC5592014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5592014) and [PMC6500492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6500492) (which of these carries each statement was not verified because PMC was blocked)
- Longitudinal stability: one study following autistic children from ages 3 to 4 into school age found sensory features persisted, independent of cognitive level and autism severity. Another (children aged 2 to 8) found typically developing children's sensory symptoms decreased over time while clinical groups showed no significant change. Cross-sectional work has found sensory hyperresponsiveness higher in older than younger children in preschool and school-age samples. Confidence: medium. Source: [PMC7491753](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7491753); [PMC9898461](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9898461); [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4zp4q2cd/qt4zp4q2cd.pdf?t=rixh8a) (exact mapping of statement to URL not verified)
- Adults: Tavassoli et al. 2014 (Autism) compared 221 autistic and 181 non-autistic adults by online self-report; autistic adults reported more sensory over-responsivity across visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory, and proprioceptive domains. Not a population prevalence study. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [CiNii record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699993770078464); [summary](https://bestpracticeautism.blogspot.com/2014/07/sensory-over-responsivity-in-adults.html)
- Persistence into adulthood with varied individual patterns (Crane et al. 2009, as cited by O'Brien and Tavassoli 2021). Confidence: medium. Source: [University of Reading repository](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/96474)
- Behavioral effect in adults: sensory input is the first-listed source of overwhelm in adult accounts of meltdowns, and adults describe avoiding triggers and retreating to safe spaces. Confidence: medium. Source: [Lewis and Stevens 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36632658/)
- Burnout lowers sensory tolerance ("reduced tolerance to stimulus"). Confidence: medium. Source: [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/)
- Sensory problems are associated with aggression in children (1,584 ATN children). Confidence: high for association. Source: [SPARK summary of Mazurek et al. 2013](https://sparkforautism.org/discover_article/understanding-aggressive-behavior-in-autism/)

### Inferences
- For synthetic data: keep sensory triggers present at all ages (do not fade them out in teens or adults). A modest peak in sensory-triggered events around ages 6 to 9 is defensible but should be labeled as an assumption.
- Shift the response type by age: 2 to 5 meltdown, covering ears, bolting, clothing refusal; 6 to 11 meltdown plus more stimming and avoidance; 12 to 17 and adult withdrawal, shutdown, leaving the setting, fatigue later the same day.
- The school-age peak coincides with entry into noisy, crowded school settings, so environment change rather than biology may explain part of it (inference; the meta-analysis cannot separate these).

### Gaps
- Mazurek et al. 2013 on sensory over-responsivity, anxiety, and GI problems (2,973 ATN children) was named in the brief but not retrieved; no figures are reported here.
- Modality-specific age trends (noise versus clothing texture versus lights versus crowds) were not found.
- No longitudinal study spanning childhood to adulthood with one consistent measure was found.
- The Ben-Sasson 2019 update's age findings were not confirmed.

## 4. What is "after-school restraint collapse", what evidence exists, and at which ages?

### Takeaway
"After-school restraint collapse" is a parenting term, not a research construct: it describes a child holding it together at school and then showing meltdown, shutdown, or irritability at home. No peer-reviewed study measuring it was found; support is indirect, from small qualitative masking studies in adolescents (mostly girls), parent surveys on school distress, and consistent practitioner and parent reports. Treat it as plausible and widely reported but weakly evidenced.

### Cited Findings
- The term was coined by counsellor and parenting educator Andrea Loewen Nair, not by a research team; it is not a diagnosis and does not appear in the DSM or, as far as the searches showed, in peer-reviewed literature as a measured outcome. Confidence: high that the term is non-clinical; medium on attribution (blog-level sources). Source: [NYU "The Nest Egg" blog](https://wp.nyu.edu/thenestegg/tag/masking); [Vizyplan blog](https://vizyplan.com/blog/after-school-restraint-collapse-autism/)
- Charity and service descriptions: Amaze (Australia) states that autistic children often mask at school, which can lead to meltdowns or shutdowns when they get home. Middletown Centre for Autism has published guidance on the pattern. Confidence: medium as a description of common reports; not prevalence. Source: [Amaze parent guide to autistic burnout](https://www.amaze.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Autistic-Burnout-Parents-Guide-A4-CMYK-FA1.0.pdf); [Middletown Centre for Autism](https://www.middletownautism.com/social-media/after-school-restraint-collapse-9-2021)
- Closest peer-reviewed support: Halsall et al. 2021 (8 autistic adolescent girls, mothers, educators): exhaustion was the main consequence of camouflaging at school, and one girl reported being more angry at home after hiding anger at school. Confidence: low to medium (tiny sample, girls only, adolescents). Source: [Autism journal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13623613211012819)
- A qualitative study of autistic girls' camouflaging in inclusive high schools ("A round, bruising sort of pain") also addresses the cost of camouflaging at school; details not retrieved. Confidence: low. Source: [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13222302/)
- Mechanism proposed by practitioners: masking is most pronounced in structured settings such as school; prolonged masking contributes to exhaustion and burnout (burnout link from Raymaker 2020, adults). Confidence: low to medium. Source: [Sensory Integration Education article](https://www.sensoryintegrationeducation.com/pages/news-masking-in-neurodivergent-children-and-young-people-the-impact-on-mental-health-and-externalising-behaviour); [Raymaker et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/)
- A blog cites a study of 733 autistic children and teens in Autism Research in which camouflaging predicted anxiety, depression, and somatic complaints. The original paper was not located. Confidence: low (secondhand). Source: [Vizyplan blog](https://vizyplan.com/blog/after-school-restraint-collapse-autism/)
- Research on teacher versus parent views is limited: authors of a trajectory study note that teacher perspectives on distress behavior have rarely been captured over time, which is part of why home versus school discrepancy is poorly documented. Confidence: medium. Source: [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.09.09.22279781.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- Ages: the pattern requires school attendance plus enough social awareness to suppress distress, so school age (6 to 11) and adolescence (12 to 17) are the bands to model. It can appear from the first school or preschool year in guidance sources, but the only research support is in adolescents. In adults the analogous pattern is collapse after work or social events, linked to burnout (inference from Raymaker 2020 and Lewis and Stevens 2023).
- For synthetic data: a school-age or adolescent persona can show a cluster of meltdown or shutdown logs in the 1 to 2 hours after school on school days, fewer on weekends and holidays, with notes like "teacher says fine at school". This should be presented in the app as a "possible thing to check", never as an established cause.
- Any UI copy should avoid implying the pattern is clinically validated.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of after-school distress timing, frequency, or prevalence in autistic children was found.
- No study comparing school-day versus non-school-day distress logs was found.
- Evidence in boys, in children with intellectual disability, and in primary-age children is essentially absent from the peer-reviewed sources found.

## 5. Time-of-day patterns: mornings before school, after school, bedtime

### Takeaway
No study was found that measures distress in autistic people by time of day. Mornings before school, the after-school period, and bedtime are consistently named in guidance and practitioner sources as high-load windows because they combine transitions, time pressure, fatigue, and sensory demands, but this is expert and caregiver consensus, not measured data.

### Cited Findings
- A targeted search found no diary or momentary-assessment study comparing distress across morning, after-school, and bedtime windows. Two related papers (a mixed-methods study on autistic children's behavior relating to time, and a family diary study) do not break distress down by time of day. Confidence: medium that the gap is real (one extended search). Source: ["No idea of time" mixed-methods study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8323338/); [diary study of family interactions](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10915142/)
- Guidance recommends logging time of day precisely because patterns by time are expected to vary by child: National Autistic Society (meltdowns can occur at particular times or places), Raising Children Network (time of day, tired, hungry, activity just before such as getting ready for school), CPCS (note patterns by time of day). Confidence: medium (guidance). Source: [National Autistic Society](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/behaviour/meltdowns/all-audiences); [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/behaviour/common-concerns/aggressive-behaviour-asd); [CPCS](https://www.cpcs.org.uk/parenting-space/understanding-the-triggers-behind-behaviour-in-autistic-children/)
- Mornings: a service-provider guide describes mornings as combining transitions, time pressure, and sensory demands, and says a small delay can cascade through the day. Practitioner commentary. Confidence: low. Source: [Wattle Blue guide](https://wattleblue.com/working-parents-guide-to-daily-routines-for-children-with-autism/)
- After school: see section 4. Confidence: low to medium.
- Bedtime: sleep problems are associated with daytime aggression, irritability, inattention, and hyperactivity (81 autistic children), and with aggression in the 1,584-child ATN sample, which supports a next-day effect of poor sleep rather than a bedtime timing claim. Confidence: medium for the sleep association. Source: [summary of sleep study](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/sleep-and-behavioral-problems-in-children-with-autism-spectrum-disorde/); [SPARK summary](https://sparkforautism.org/discover_article/understanding-aggressive-behavior-in-autism/)

### Inferences
- Reasonable synthetic time-of-day shapes (assumptions, to be labeled as such):
  - 2 to 5: spread across the day, with peaks before meals and naps (hunger, tiredness), at transitions (leaving the house, leaving the playground), and at bedtime.
  - 6 to 11: weekday morning peak (dressing, clothing texture, leaving for school), a larger after-school peak, and a bedtime peak tied to screen time ending.
  - 12 to 17: smaller morning peak that may show up as refusal or shutdown, after-school and evening withdrawal, late bedtime friction.
  - Adult: after work or after social events, and evenings; less tied to clock time, more to cumulative load.
- Weekday versus weekend contrast is likely a stronger and more demo-friendly signal than clock time alone for school-age personas.
- A "night before: poor sleep" flag raising next-day event probability is better supported than any specific clock-time effect.

### Gaps
- No quantitative time-of-day data for any age band.
- No data on bedtime distress frequency by age was retrieved here (sleep is covered by another researcher).
- No evidence found on morning school-refusal timing specifically in autistic children beyond the school distress survey.

## 6. How does spoken-language level or intellectual disability interact with age in these patterns?

### Takeaway
Language and IQ explain less of early-childhood distress than commonly assumed, but intellectual disability strongly shapes the trajectory: outward distress (aggression, self-injury, property damage) is more common and more persistent with moderate to profound intellectual disability, and it improves less with age. Speaking autistic people without intellectual disability are the group in which masking, internalized distress, shutdown, and burnout are documented, largely because the qualitative studies only sampled them.

### Cited Findings
- Preschool: speech, language comprehension, and IQ explained under 3% of tantrum variance in 240 autistic children aged 15 to 71 months; non-speaking children with adequate cognitive capacity did not have more tantrums than speaking children; children speaking at about a 2 year old level had more tantrums than those with less speech. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [Penn State news](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/speech-and-language-deficits-children-autism-may-not-cause-tantrums)
- Children and adolescents: the role of IQ in aggression remains unclear across studies (Hill et al. 2014; Dominick et al. 2007, as characterized in a later review). One large sample found no significant difference in parent-rated distress behavior between autistic children with average cognition and those with below-average cognition. Confidence: medium. Source: [Springer, JADD 2019 review](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10803-019-04129-3); [Springer, JADD 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-023-06123-2)
- Kanne and Mazurek 2011 reported that IQ and language level did not predict aggression in 1,380 children aged 4 to 17 [R]. Confidence: low until rechecked. Source: [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10803-010-1118-4)
- Severity of intellectual disability: moderate to profound intellectual disability is associated with higher prevalence of self-injury, aggression, and destructive behavior than mild intellectual disability. Confidence: high. Source: [Davies and Oliver 2016, University of Birmingham](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/27171542/Davies_Oliver_2016_Self_injury_aggression_and_destruction_in_SID_RDD.pdf)
- Persistence: in 417 children with severe intellectual disability rated by teachers 15 to 18 months apart, aggression, destruction, and self-injury persisted in 69%, 57%, and 58% respectively. Not autism-specific. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [Davies and Oliver 2016](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/27171542/Davies_Oliver_2016_Self_injury_aggression_and_destruction_in_SID_RDD.pdf); [Aston University repository](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/document/90512)
- Autism plus intellectual disability: a meta-analysis of 22 studies found autistic children and adults more likely than others with intellectual disability to show self-injury and aggression; a separate study of over 3,000 adults with intellectual disability found no relationship with autism. Conflicting. Confidence: medium. Source: [journal record on repetitive, self-injurious, and aggressive behavior](https://thim.mijn.bsl.nl/the-association-between-repetitive-self-injurious-and-aggressive/552688); [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-1-4614-3037-7_3)
- General prevalence of such behavior in people with intellectual disability: about 5% to 15%. Confidence: medium. Source: [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-1-4614-3037-7_3)
- Adolescents and adults: co-occurring intellectual disability was linked to more autism features and distress behaviors and less improvement over 4.5 years (241 people aged 10 to 52). Confidence: high. Source: [Washington University release on Shattuck et al. 2007](https://source.washu.edu/2007/09/study-shows-autism-symptoms-can-improve-into-adulthood/)
- Ages 9 to 18: children with more pronounced core autism features had consistently higher irritability and hyperactivity over time; youths with less pronounced features became markedly more socially withdrawn. Confidence: medium. Source: [Anderson et al. 2011, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3185304); [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/teenage-wasteland/)
- Sampling limit: adult meltdown, shutdown, and burnout studies used written or spoken interviews, and the meltdown study's authors note the format may exclude people who communicate in other ways. Confidence: high. Source: [Lewis and Stevens 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36632658/)
- Pain and illness as drivers in people with limited speech: only a title-level hit was found (Carr and Owen-DeSchryver 2007 on physical illness, pain, and behavior in minimally verbal people with developmental disabilities). Confidence: low (not read). Source: [Springer chapter listing](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-030-88538-0_3)

### Inferences
- For synthetic personas, model two broad trajectories rather than one:
  - Speaking, no intellectual disability: outward meltdowns decline from early childhood; from late primary age, more masking at school, after-school collapse, shutdown, withdrawal; burnout risk in teens and adults.
  - Minimal speech or intellectual disability: outward distress (including aggression toward others) stays more frequent and stable into adolescence and adulthood; triggers are harder for caregivers to identify, so logs should contain more "unknown trigger" entries and more reliance on context fields (sleep, hunger, setting, time).
- Do not model non-speaking young children as having automatically more meltdowns than speaking peers; Mayes 2017 argues against it. Emerging speech (about 2 year level) may coincide with a tantrum peak.
- Because distress in people with limited speech can signal pain or illness, a sudden change in pattern in such a persona should route to the app's "contact your doctor or care team" red-flag path rather than a trigger suggestion (consistent with project guardrails; pain prevalence is covered by another researcher).
- Shutdown and burnout are probably under-recorded, not absent, in people with intellectual disability; the evidence base simply has not sampled them.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly tests the interaction of age band with language level on distress type.
- No data on shutdown, masking, or burnout in autistic people with intellectual disability or minimal speech.
- Hill 2014 findings on nonverbal IQ and language were not retrieved (full text blocked).
- AAP 2020 clinical report content on co-occurring intellectual disability and behavior was not retrieved.
- Network limitation: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov need to be allowed in the session's network settings to verify primary figures marked [R] and to pull per-age-band tables.
