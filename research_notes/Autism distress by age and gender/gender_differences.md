# Sex and gender differences in how distress presents in autistic children, adolescents, and adults

Method note for the report writer: findings below come from web search result summaries dated 2026-11-14. Full-text fetching was blocked in this session (the host discovery.ucl.ac.uk could not be resolved, so no full papers were opened). Every number was reported by a search summary of the linked source; none was checked against the full paper. Items marked "secondhand" were reported by a source other than the original paper. Confidence levels: high = large study or meta-analysis with consistent replication; medium = single good study, or meta-analysis with high heterogeneity; low = small, qualitative, non-peer-reviewed, or secondhand. Nothing here is diagnostic. All content is for designing synthetic data and bias checks in a caregiver logging app.

## 1. Diagnosed prevalence ratio by sex and age at diagnosis

### Takeaway
The latest CDC figure is 3.4 boys identified per girl at age 8 (2022 data), and the best meta-analytic estimate of the underlying ratio is closer to 3:1 than 4:1, which implies some autistic girls are not being identified. Girls are identified later (roughly 1.5 years later in several cohorts), and the sex ratio narrows sharply in adolescence and adulthood.

### Cited Findings
- CDC ADDM Network, surveillance year 2022 (published April 2025, 16 US sites, 8-year-olds): overall prevalence 32.2 per 1,000 (1 in 31), up from 1 in 36 in 2020. Boys 49.2 per 1,000, girls 14.3 per 1,000, ratio 3.4 to 1. Site range 9.7 (Laredo, Texas) to 53.1 (California) per 1,000. Sample: population surveillance, not nationally representative. Confidence: high. Source: [CDC MMWR Surveillance Summaries 74(SS-2), Shaw et al. 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/ss/ss7402a1.htm); [CDC summary page](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/articles/prevalence-varies-across-us-communities.html)
- Same ADDM cycle: ratio among 4-year-olds was 2.8 to 1 versus 3.4 to 1 among 8-year-olds. Confidence: medium (reported by a university press summary). Source: [Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health](https://publichealth.jhu.edu/2025/new-cdc-report-shows-increase-in-autism-in-2022-with-notable-shifts-in-race-ethnicity-and-sex)
- Loomes, Hull and Mandy 2017 (systematic review and meta-analysis, 54 studies, 13,784,284 participants, 53,712 autistic): the male to female ratio among children meeting criteria is closer to 3:1 than the commonly assumed 4:1. Confidence: high. Source: [UCL Discovery record for Loomes et al. 2017](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1558343); [IACC 2017 Summary of Advances](https://iacc.hhs.gov/publications/summary-of-advances/2017/question7.shtml) (reports 3:1 to 3.5:1)
- Loomes subgroup figures (secondhand, not verified against the paper): overall pooled ratio 4.2:1; 4.56:1 in studies that only counted already diagnosed participants; 3.32:1 in high-quality studies; about 3.1:1 in studies with a high proportion of children with intellectual disability. The gap between passive (diagnosed only) and active case-finding studies is the core evidence that girls are under-identified. Confidence: medium. Source: [Embrace Autism summary](https://embrace-autism.com/the-autism-sex-ratio/); [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/estimate-autisms-sex-ratio-reaches-new-low/)
- Epic Research analysis of electronic health records (over 338,000 patients first diagnosed 2015 to 2024, US, not peer reviewed): girls' median age at diagnosis stayed at about 8; boys' median fell from 7 (2015) to 5 (2024). About one third of girls versus nearly half of boys were diagnosed before age 5. One in four women versus about one in eight men were first diagnosed at 19 or older. Confidence: medium to low (large sample, not peer reviewed, media report). Source: [ABC News / Good Morning America, Aug 2025](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/news/story/autism-diagnoses-happening-earlier-boys-girls-wait-years-124586710)
- Rhode Island RI-CART cohort (Autism Research): girls were diagnosed on average nearly 1.5 years later than boys. Confidence: medium. Source: [Neuroscience News coverage](https://neurosciencenews.com/asd-female-cooccurring-disorders-15538)
- Large Swedish population cohort followed over about 35 years (reported as BMJ, 2026): about three boys per girl among those diagnosed before age 10; the ratio shrinks to about 1.2 to 1 by age 20. The study did not test why. Confidence: medium (secondhand, via news coverage). Source: [News-Medical, Feb 2026](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260209/Why-autism-is-diagnosed-later-in-females-than-males.aspx); related earlier coverage [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/sex-bias-in-autism-drops-as-age-at-diagnosis-rises/)
- Contested: an electronic health records study (Gu et al. 2023, as cited in search results) found the sex difference in diagnosis age was reduced and no longer statistically significant after controlling for co-occurring conditions; another study found sex was not the strongest predictor of diagnosis age. Confidence: low to medium (secondhand). Source: [PMC8023413](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8023413/)
- Dworzynski et al. 2012 (JAACAP, UK twin population sample, age 10 to 12): among children with similarly high autistic trait scores, girls who met diagnostic criteria had significantly more additional difficulties (low intellectual level, behavioral difficulties) than girls who did not; boys showed no such difference. Authors conclude that without additional intellectual or behavioral difficulties, girls are less likely than boys to meet criteria. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [The Transmitter coverage of Dworzynski et al. 2012](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/diagnosis-eludes-many-girls-with-autism-study-says/)
- Catherine Lord (not involved in the Epic analysis): girls are often diagnosed later, and girls with milder presentation are sometimes missed entirely. Expert opinion. Confidence: medium. Source: [ABC News / GMA](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/news/story/autism-diagnoses-happening-earlier-boys-girls-wait-years-124586710)

### Inferences
- Synthetic profiles should not assume a 4:1 ratio. Roughly 3:1 for children is defensible, moving toward parity for adolescents and adults.
- Girls in a caregiver app are more likely to be late identified or not formally identified. The app should not require a diagnosis and should not treat "no diagnosis" as "no needs".
- Because girls who do get identified tend to have more visible co-occurring difficulties, datasets built from diagnosed girls over-represent externalizing presentations in girls. Synthetic data should deliberately include quiet, internalized profiles for girls.

### Gaps
- The ADDM 2022 report's median age at earliest diagnosis by sex was not retrieved. A secondary summary gives an average age at first diagnosis of 47 months for 5,887 children, without a sex split; check the MMWR text.
- No peer-reviewed, nationally representative median age at diagnosis by sex was confirmed. The commonly cited "1.5 years later" comes from regional cohorts.
- The exact primary URL for the 2026 Swedish cohort paper was not confirmed.

## 2. Masking and camouflaging: prevalence, intensity, costs, and the school versus home pattern

### Takeaway
Autistic girls and women on average report more camouflaging than autistic boys and men, but the difference is modest, not universal, and about a third of studies find no sex difference; no study finds males camouflage more. Camouflaging is linked to anxiety, depression, exhaustion, and burnout (correlational evidence), and qualitative studies and one parent versus teacher rating study support a pattern of holding it together at school with distress showing at home.

### Cited Findings
- Hull et al. 2020 (Autism; online survey of autistic and non-autistic adults using the CAT-Q): autistic females scored higher than autistic males on the masking and assimilation subscales but not on compensation. Non-autistic males and females did not differ on any subscale. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [Hull et al. 2020, UCL Discovery accepted manuscript](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10080352/1/Hull%20AAM%20Paper%20proof3609.pdf)
- Systematic review of camouflaging studies: 18 studies examined sex or gender differences; 6 found no difference; none found that autistic males camouflage more than autistic females. Cage and Troxell-Whitman 2019 is an example of a null result. Confidence: medium (URL to claim mapping from search listing, not verified in full text). Source: [PMC9902997](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9902997/)
- Milner et al. 2023 (Autism Research, adults): the relationship between camouflaging and later age at autism diagnosis was stronger for females than males. Confidence: medium. Source: [Milner et al. 2023, UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10183799/1/Hull_Autism%20Research%20-%202023%20-%20Milner%20-%20Does%20camouflaging%20predict%20age%20.pdf)
- Milner et al. 2024 (comparison of self-report and discrepancy measures): diagnosed females scored higher than diagnosed males on all camouflaging measures, but there was no sex difference in a high-trait undiagnosed group; the authors suggest mixed evidence partly reflects the measure used. Confidence: medium. Source: [PMC10946751](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10946751/)
- Costs, meta-analysis: Khudiakova et al. 2024 (systematic review, 16 studies pooled, random effects): significant moderate positive correlations between camouflaging and anxiety, depression, and social anxiety, and a small negative correlation with wellbeing. Evidence is cross-sectional; causality is not established. Confidence: medium to high for association, low for causation. Source: [University of Birmingham research portal](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-mental-health-outcomes-a/)
- Costs, reviews: a 2023 systematic review reports camouflaging is associated with unmet needs, delayed diagnosis, and autistic burnout; qualitative syntheses describe a theme of being "too drained to disguise". Confidence: medium. Source: [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1108110)
- School versus home, qualitative: foundational studies (Bargiela et al. 2016; Tierney et al. 2016, as summarized in a later paper) found adolescent and adult autistic females camouflaged at school or work and stopped on returning home, and exhaustion from camouflaging was often linked to distress and difficult moments at home. Confidence: medium (small qualitative samples, secondhand summary). Source: [Springer 2026 paper summarizing this work](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s41252-026-00476-2)
- Halsall, Clarke and Crane 2021 (8 adolescent autistic girls in resource classes attached to mainstream schools, plus parents and staff): girls tried to camouflage both autism and learning needs, especially in mainstream classrooms; the attempts often did not succeed; staff expressed surprise at the level of social and emotional distress the girls experienced. Confidence: low to medium (small qualitative study). Source: [PMC8419293](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8419293)
- School versus home, quantitative: Putnam et al. 2024 (J Autism Dev Disord, sex-matched sample of 388 autistic youth, Social Responsiveness Scale): no sex difference in parent ratings, but teachers rated boys higher than girls on total score and all domains. Parents of girls reported significantly more autistic traits than teachers did. Being older and being female predicted a larger parent versus teacher gap. Authors interpret this as possible camouflaging at school; this is an interpretation, not a tested mechanism. Confidence: medium. Source: [Putnam et al. 2024 record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/e/mdl-39060706); [plain-language summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=70828)
- Practice sources describe girls who "keep it together" at school and have frequent meltdowns or shutdowns at home. The label "after-school restraint collapse" appears on parenting sites; no study defining or validating that term was found. Confidence: low (clinical opinion). Source: [Place2Be educational psychologist blog](https://place2be.org.uk/about-us/news-and-blogs/2017/september/masking-meltdowns-and-misdiagnosis); [Attwood and Garnett](https://www.attwoodandgarnettevents.com/blogs/news/recognising-and-understanding-autistic-girls-at-school)

### Inferences
- A cluster of distress logs in the first one to two hours after school, with calm teacher reports, is a plausible pattern to represent in synthetic data, more often (not exclusively) for girls and more with increasing age. The app could offer "possible thing to check: was today a high-effort social day?" without asserting a cause.
- "No problems reported at school" should never be used to down-weight a caregiver's home logs.
- Boys camouflage too. Any rule that links masking only to girls would itself be biased. Model masking as a per-person trait with a sex-shifted distribution and large overlap.
- The age gradient (larger parent versus teacher gap in older girls) suggests the school and home discrepancy grows through late primary and secondary school.

### Gaps
- No pooled effect size for the sex difference in CAT-Q scores was retrieved.
- Almost all CAT-Q data are from adults who can self-report; there is very little camouflaging data for young children or for autistic people with intellectual disability or minimal speech.
- No large quantitative study directly measuring time-of-day distress at home after school was found.
- Raymaker et al. 2020 (autistic burnout definition study) was searched for but not retrieved; burnout claims rest on the reviews above.

## 3. Internalizing versus externalizing presentation by sex

### Takeaway
The popular picture (girls internalize, boys externalize) is only weakly supported: the newest meta-analysis finds no significant sex difference in overall internalizing or depression among autistic children and young people, and only a small anxiety difference (girls higher, most clearly in older girls and those with higher IQ). Externalizing differences are usually not found in autistic samples, and some clinic studies even find more irritability in girls, probably because the girls who get diagnosed are those with more visible difficulties.

### Cited Findings
- Suominen et al. 2025 (JAACAP, systematic review and meta-analysis; 56 studies; 13,410 autistic children and young people: 3,657 girls, 9,753 boys): overall internalizing g = 0.10 (95% CI -0.04 to 0.23, p = 0.148, not significant); depression g = 0.12 (95% CI -0.01 to 0.25, p = 0.067, not significant). Girls showed more anxiety than boys, most pronounced in older girls and those with higher IQ. Heterogeneity was high for all pooled estimates. Confidence: medium to high (large meta-analysis, high heterogeneity). Source: [Suominen et al. 2025, UCL Discovery record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10216243) (DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2025.10.010)
- Earlier evidence was sparse and inconsistent: one earlier meta-analysis found only eight studies on sex differences in internalizing among autistic children, with mixed results. Individual studies range from no difference in either domain to greater total and externalizing difficulties, irritability, and lethargy in girls. Confidence: medium. Source: [Molecular Autism 2020 article](https://molecularautism.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s13229-020-00380-z); [Sex Differences in Externalizing and Internalizing Symptoms of Children with ASD](https://mijn.bsl.nl/sex-differences-in-externalizing-and-internalizing-symptoms-of-c/16901308)
- Externalizing: sex differences in externalizing among autistic children are typically not found, in contrast with the general population where boys score higher. Confidence: medium (statement from search summary of the literature, no pooled estimate retrieved). Source: [Sex Differences in Externalizing and Internalizing Symptoms in ASD](https://antoniusacademie.mijn.bsl.nl/sex-differences-in-externalizing-and-internalizing-symptoms-in-a/17817744)
- Ascertainment effect: girls who meet criteria carry more additional behavioral or intellectual difficulties than equally high-trait girls who do not (Dworzynski et al. 2012). This means diagnosed samples can under-represent quiet, internalizing girls. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/diagnosis-eludes-many-girls-with-autism-study-says/)
- Adolescence: a UCL study found emotional and peer difficulties tend to increase in autistic young people across adolescence, with contributions that varied by sex and by timing of diagnosis, and with school adjustment and bullying playing a role. Confidence: medium (specific estimates not retrieved). Source: [UCL Discovery, Dawe-Lane](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10204454/1/DaweLane.pdf)
- Self-injury by sex, sleep, and elopement are covered by another researcher. Brief note only: one clinic study cited above reported more self-injurious behavior in girls than boys, but this is a single mixed finding. Confidence: low. Source: [Sex Differences in Externalizing and Internalizing Symptoms of Children with ASD](https://mijn.bsl.nl/sex-differences-in-externalizing-and-internalizing-symptoms-of-c/16901308)

### Inferences
- Do not hard-code "girls withdraw, boys have meltdowns". The defensible design is: every distress type is available for every person; the only sex-linked shift with reasonable support is somewhat higher anxiety in older and more verbal girls.
- The real bias risk is detection, not prevalence. Quiet distress (withdrawal, going silent, shutdown, stomach aches, refusing food, staying in a room) is less salient to an observer than shouting or hitting, so it is less likely to be logged for anyone. Voice prompts and vocabulary should name quiet distress types as first-class events.
- Because parent report shows smaller sex differences than expected, a caregiver logging app (parent-informant) may be better placed than school reports to capture distress in girls.

### Gaps
- The exact pooled g for anxiety in Suominen et al. 2025 was not retrieved (full text blocked).
- No pooled effect size for externalizing sex differences in autistic samples was found.
- No sex-stratified prevalence was found for shutdown, withdrawal, or somatic complaints specifically (as distinct from broadband internalizing scores). These distinctions rest on clinical description, not measurement.
- Informant moderation (parent versus self versus teacher) in Suominen et al. was not retrieved.

## 4. Restricted and repetitive behaviors, sensory sensitivities, and special interests

### Takeaway
Autistic girls show somewhat fewer of the visible, stereotyped repetitive behaviors and classic restricted interests, but do not differ from boys on insistence on sameness or sensory differences at the meta-analytic level (some single studies find greater sensory reactivity in girls and women). Girls' interests more often look age and gender typical in content (animals, art, fictional characters), which makes intensity easier to miss.

### Cited Findings
- Van Wijngaarden-Cremers et al. 2014 (meta-analysis, 22 studies): females showed on average fewer restricted and repetitive behaviors and interests than males on overall ADI-R and ADOS scores. Limitation: could not account for intellectual disability, and higher-IQ girls may have been missing from samples. Confidence: medium. Source: [review of sex differences in RRBIs, Simply Psychology summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=67182)
- Edwards et al. 2023 (meta-analysis splitting RRBIs into narrow constructs): males had significantly higher stereotyped behaviors and restricted interests; no sex difference for sensory experiences or insistence on sameness. Overall pooled difference was not significant (SMD = -0.09, 95% CI -0.27 to 0.09, secondhand figure). Significant heterogeneity. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [University of Southampton ePrints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/480499/); [PubMed 37485556](https://www.pubmed.gov/37485556/)
- Sensory, children: Osorio et al. 2021 (Autism Research; 168 autistic children, only 26 girls, and 439 typically developing children): the sex gap in sensory processing was larger in autistic than typically developing children, with autistic girls showing more hearing and balance or motion sensitivities. Confidence: low to medium (small number of girls). Source: [PMC9290069](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9290069)
- Sensory, adults: Lai et al. 2011 (IQ-matched adults, three ADI-R sensory items): autistic women reported more lifetime sensory difficulties than autistic men. Other studies find no sex difference. Overall evidence is mixed, leaning toward greater reported sensory reactivity in females. Confidence: low to medium (secondhand). Source: [cited in PMC9290069](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9290069); null example [Could sensory differences be a sex-indifferent biomarker of autism?](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/could-sensory-differences-be-a-sex-indifferent-biomarker-of-auti/23634020)
- Special interests: Brown et al. 2024 (caregiver report, 1,921 autistic youth, SPARK cohort): most interest categories showed no sex difference; where differences existed they followed conventional lines (more boys: construction, math, mechanical things; more girls: animals, arts and crafts). Boys had slightly more special interests; no difference in age of onset or duration. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [SPARK Research Match summary](https://sparkforautism.org/discover_article/rm-interests-gender/)
- Nowell et al. 2019 (secondhand): autistic girls more often have interests in dolls, animals, celebrities, arts and crafts, or fictional characters; boys more often object-centered interests such as trains or construction. Reviews note girls' interests are more likely to be age and gender normative in topic, so unusual intensity is overlooked. Confidence: medium. Source: [Autism Spectrum News](https://autismspectrumnews.org/when-special-interests-look-typical-understanding-the-hidden-intensity-in-autistic-girls/)

### Inferences
- Sensory triggers (noise, clothing, crowds, smells) should be equally available and equally weighted for girls and boys in the trigger vocabulary. There is no evidence base for assuming girls have fewer sensory triggers; if anything the opposite.
- Insistence on sameness (distress at change of routine or transition) is a sex-neutral trigger.
- For girls, disruption of an interest that looks ordinary (a favorite show, animals, drawing) can be as significant a trigger as disruption of a stereotypically "autistic" interest. The app should let caregivers log "interrupted favorite activity" without judging the topic.
- Less visible repetitive movement in girls means stimming-based early warning signs may be subtler (hair twirling, skin picking, doodling). This is an inference from the lower stereotyped-behavior scores plus camouflaging literature, not a directly measured finding.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study was found on whether subtle repetitive behaviors in girls precede distress.
- Hiller et al. 2014 and McFayden et al. 2019 were searched for but not retrieved.
- Sensory findings by sex rely on small female samples; a dedicated sensory sex-difference meta-analysis beyond Edwards et al. was not found.

## 5. Puberty and menstruation

### Takeaway
Autistic people who menstruate report that periods amplify sensory load and make regulating emotion and behavior harder, and parents report more distress in the days before a period; the evidence is consistent but based on small surveys and qualitative studies. Evidence for puberty-specific change in autistic boys is thin; most claims about aggression rising at puberty come from non-peer-reviewed sources.

### Cited Findings
- Steward et al. 2018 (J Autism Dev Disord 48(12):4287 to 4292; online survey, 459 respondents, analysis of 123 autistic and 114 non-autistic post-menarcheal participants; convenience sample): autistic respondents shared many experiences with non-autistic respondents but also reported autism-related challenges, including heightened sensory differences and more difficulty regulating emotion and behavior during periods, with a significant negative impact on daily life. Confidence: medium (self-selected sample, qualitative). Source: [Steward et al. 2018, UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050187/9/Steward_2018_Article_LifeIsMuchMoreDifficultToManag%20(1).pdf)
- Steward's National Autistic Society summary: specific sensory difficulties include odors, the sensation of blood, period products, and using public toilets; respondents wanted more detailed, accurate advance information about periods. Confidence: medium. Source: [National Autistic Society, Autistic people and menstruation](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/professional-practice/menstruation)
- Steward et al. also note earlier studies suggesting higher rates of premenstrual symptoms in autistic women, with cause unknown. Confidence: low to medium (no rate retrieved). Source: [Steward et al. 2018](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050187/9/Steward_2018_Article_LifeIsMuchMoreDifficultToManag%20(1).pdf)
- Cummins, Pellicano and Crane (published online 2018, print 2020; interviews with 10 parents of 9 minimally verbal autistic girls with intellectual disability currently in puberty, plus 10 educators in UK and Ireland special schools): for most girls puberty was felt to go well and they coped with the changes, despite parents' considerable worry beforehand. Themes: wide individual variation in experience and needs; promoting dignity and respect; practical ways to support. Confidence: low to medium (very small qualitative sample). Source: [PMC7308246](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7308246); [CRAE project page](https://crae.ioe.ac.uk/portfolio/puberty-and-menstruation-in-autistic-women-and-girls/)
- A later occupational therapy review cites Cummins et al. for parents reporting more distress behaviors in the days before menstruation. A systematic review of qualitative menstruation studies notes that parents and professionals focus on external behavior (hygiene, independence), while autistic people themselves emphasize internal experience (pain, sensory load, emotion). Confidence: low to medium (secondhand). Source: [AJOT 2025 review](https://sage.cnpereading.com/doi/10.5014/ajot.2025.051141); [PMC7617708](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7617708)
- Boys: no rigorous study was found showing a puberty-specific rise in aggression in autistic boys. Parenting websites assert it without primary data. In non-autistic children (135 children aged 8 to 13), early or late pubertal timing in boys, but not girls, was linked to more antisocial behavior. Confidence: low for autistic boys. Source: [News-Medical coverage of the non-autistic study](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20100504/Early-or-delayed-puberty-can-trigger-antisocial-behavior-in-adolescent-boys.aspx)
- Both sexes: emotional and peer difficulties tend to rise through adolescence in autistic young people, with school adjustment and bullying as contributors. Confidence: medium. Source: [UCL Discovery, Dawe-Lane](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10204454/1/DaweLane.pdf)

### Inferences
- A menstrual cycle context tag (optional, caregiver-entered, for any person who menstruates) is a justified "possible thing to check" for recurring monthly clusters of distress, sensory overload, or pain signals. It should be optional and never inferred from a sex field.
- Period pain is a pain signal. Under CareSignal's guardrails, signs of pain or illness route to "Please contact your doctor or care team" first. Cycle-linked distress should not be normalized to the point of hiding a red flag.
- Because caregivers tend to log external behavior while the person experiences internal load, prompts such as "any signs of discomfort, pain, or sensory overload?" may capture what would otherwise be missed, especially for minimally verbal girls.
- For boys, the safest synthetic-data assumption is a general adolescent rise in emotional and peer-related distress, not a sex-specific aggression surge.

### Gaps
- No quantitative estimate (effect size or prevalence) of cycle-linked change in distress or behavior in autistic girls was found. Premenstrual symptom rates were not retrieved.
- No data on age at menarche or pubertal timing differences in autistic girls was retrieved in this session.
- No peer-reviewed data on puberty-related behavior change specifically in autistic boys was found.

## 6. Eating: restrictive eating, ARFID, and the autism and anorexia overlap

### Takeaway
Autism is substantially over-represented among people with restrictive eating disorders: roughly 20 to 35% of women with anorexia in clinical samples meet autism criteria (pooled mean about 23% across eating disorder populations), and about 11% of autistic people have ARFID with about 16% of people with ARFID being autistic. Estimates vary widely with method, and rates are lower in adolescent samples using parent-report developmental history.

### Cited Findings
- Westwood and Tchanturia 2017 (updated literature review, Current Psychiatry Reports): studies consistently find over-representation of autistic traits in anorexia nervosa; an earlier systematic review put mean autism prevalence in eating disorder populations at about 23% (22.9%); wide variation in tools and populations makes true prevalence hard to determine; studies of young people using parent report find lower rates than adult studies. Confidence: medium. Source: [PubMed 28540593](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28540593/)
- A clinical paper cites a review estimating that 20 to 35% of women with anorexia nervosa also meet autism criteria. Autistica states more conservatively that more than 1 in 5 women with anorexia are autistic. Confidence: medium (clinical samples, not population-based). Source: [PMC9544670](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9544670); [Autistica, anorexia and autism](https://www.autistica.org.uk/what-is-autism/signs-and-symptoms/anorexia-and-autism)
- Meta-analysis of 7 studies using the Autism-Spectrum Quotient: people with anorexia score significantly higher on autistic traits than controls; authors caution that self-report during illness needs confirmation with clinical measures. Confidence: medium. Source: [PubMed 26542816](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26542816/)
- Contested: whether elevated autistic traits in anorexia reflect underlying autism or partly the effects of starvation and illness state remains debated. Confidence: medium. Source: [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1837078/full)
- ARFID: Sader et al. 2025 (Int J Eating Disorders, prevalence meta-analysis, 21 studies, n = 7,442): autism prevalence among people with ARFID 16.27% (95% CI 8.64 to 28.53%); ARFID prevalence among autistic groups 11.41% (95% CI 2.89 to 35.76%, only 3 studies). Gender and ethnicity were significant sources of heterogeneity. Confidence: medium (wide intervals). Source: [PMC11891632](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11891632/)
- A 2023 review of ARFID in children and adolescents reported autism co-occurrence ranging from 8.2% to 54.75% across studies. Confidence: low to medium (range, secondhand). Source: [National Elf Service summary of autism and restrictive eating disorders](https://www.nationalelfservice.net/publication-types/systematic-review/autism-restrictive-eating-disorders/)
- Autistica: some autistic people are given an anorexia diagnosis when ARFID fits better, though both can co-occur; autistic restrictive eating is often driven by sensory sensitivities, need for sameness, and difficulty sensing hunger, not primarily by weight or shape concerns. Confidence: medium (charity guidance drawing on research). Source: [Autistica, eating and autism](https://www.autistica.org.uk/what-is-autism/eating-eating-disorders-and-autism)

### Inferences
- Food-related events (refusing meals, narrowing range of accepted foods, distress at mealtimes, distress at food textures or smells) belong in the logging vocabulary for all ages and sexes, framed as needs and triggers (sensory, routine, anxiety), never as non-compliance.
- Adolescent girls are the highest-risk group for restrictive eating that goes unrecognized as autism-related. A sustained pattern of meal refusal or weight loss is a health concern: the app should route to "Please contact your doctor or care team" and never offer its own interpretation, strategy ranking, or diagnosis label such as ARFID or anorexia.
- ARFID in autistic children is not strongly female-skewed in the way anorexia is, so selective eating should not be modelled as a girl-specific pattern.

### Gaps
- No population-based prevalence of anorexia among autistic adolescent girls (the reverse direction) was retrieved.
- The sex split within autistic ARFID was not retrieved.
- The Creese 2023 thesis meta-analysis (24 studies) pooled estimate was not retrievable.

## 7. Gender diversity in autistic people and what it means for a sex field

### Takeaway
Gender diversity is clearly elevated among autistic people: transgender and gender-diverse adults are 3 to 6 times as likely as cisgender adults to report an autism diagnosis, and one meta-analysis summary puts autism at roughly 11 times more common in gender-diverse than general populations. A single binary sex field will misdescribe a meaningful minority of autistic people.

### Cited Findings
- Warrier et al. 2020 (Nature Communications; five cross-sectional datasets, 641,860 participants, 30,892 autistic, 3,777 gender diverse): transgender and gender-diverse adults were 3 to 6 times as likely as cisgender adults to report an autism diagnosis, and scored higher on autistic trait measures regardless of diagnosis. Limits: self-reported diagnosis, cross-sectional, and most data came from one online survey linked to a 2017 TV documentary, so not representative. Confidence: medium to high for direction, medium for magnitude. Source: [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/largest-study-to-date-confirms-overlap-between-autism-and-gender-diversity/); [NIHR Cambridge BRC](https://cambridgebrc.nihr.ac.uk/2020/08/07/transgender-and-gender-diverse-individuals-are-more-likely-to-be-autistic-and-report-higher-autistic-traits/)
- Kallitsounaki and Williams 2023 (J Autism Dev Disord 53:3103 to 3117; systematic review and meta-analysis spanning child, adolescent, and adult studies): cited by the same authors as showing autism diagnoses are about 11 times more common in gender-diverse people than in the general population (secondhand figure; exact estimate and interval not verified). Confidence: medium. Source: [Kent Academic Repository](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/95954)
- Youth: in a study of autistic and non-autistic youth and caregivers, parents of autistic children reported higher body incongruence than parents of non-autistic children, particularly for children assigned female at birth; autistic children self-reported feeling less like their birth-assigned sex and more often nonbinary. Confidence: low to medium (single study). Source: [City, University of London repository](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/36847/)
- Contested: not all studies agree. Mo et al. 2024 is cited as finding no group difference, and the overlap is described as disputed in parts of the literature. Confidence: low (secondhand). Source: [SAGE Autism 2025 article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/13623613251379920)

### Inferences
- Data model: do not use one required binary "sex" field. A reasonable minimum is an optional gender field (girl or woman, boy or man, nonbinary, self-described, prefer not to say) plus separate optional body-relevant context flags (for example "menstruates") that are entered directly by the caregiver and never derived from gender.
- No distress interpretation, prompt wording, or strategy ranking should branch on the gender field. If gender is used at all, use it only for bias auditing (for example checking that quiet distress is logged at comparable rates across groups).
- Synthetic data should include some nonbinary and transgender autistic people so the UI and statistics are tested on them. With the 5-family minimum for community statistics, small gender groups will often be suppressed; the UI should handle that without singling anyone out.
- Much of the sex-difference literature above conflates sex assigned at birth and gender and excludes gender-diverse participants, so it should be applied loosely.

### Gaps
- A direct prevalence of gender diversity among autistic children (percent of autistic youth who are gender diverse) was not retrieved; figures here run mostly in the other direction (autism among gender-diverse people).
- No evidence was found on how distress presentation or camouflaging differs for transgender and nonbinary autistic people.

## 8. Reporting bias: do parents and teachers rate the same behaviors differently by sex?

### Takeaway
Yes, there is evidence that what gets noticed depends on who is rating: teachers rate autistic girls as having fewer autistic traits than boys while parents of the same children do not, and girls appear to need more visible additional difficulties before they are identified. Whether this reflects true context differences (camouflaging at school), rater expectations, or both is unresolved.

### Cited Findings
- Putnam et al. 2024 (388 sex-matched autistic youth, SRS): no sex difference on parent ratings; teachers rated boys higher on total and all domain scores; parents of girls rated more traits than teachers; older age and female sex predicted larger parent versus teacher discrepancy. Confidence: medium. Source: [Putnam et al. 2024 record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/e/mdl-39060706); [summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=70828)
- Authors' interpretation: girls may be overlooked by teachers because of deliberate behavior changes at school (camouflaging) or gender-based expectations. Not directly tested. Confidence: low to medium. Source: [summary of Putnam et al. 2024](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=70828)
- Dworzynski et al. 2012: at equal autistic trait levels, girls who were diagnosed had more additional behavioral or intellectual difficulties than undiagnosed high-trait girls; no such gap in boys. Consistent with a higher recognition threshold for girls; an alternative reading is that undiagnosed high-trait girls cope better. Confidence: medium to high. Source: [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/diagnosis-eludes-many-girls-with-autism-study-says/)
- Staff surprise: in Halsall et al. 2021, school staff expressed surprise at the extent of autistic girls' social and emotional distress, consistent with under-recognition in school. Confidence: low to medium (small qualitative). Source: [PMC8419293](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8419293)
- Menstruation literature shows a related informant gap: parents and professionals report on external behavior while autistic people report internal experience. Confidence: low to medium. Source: [PMC7617708](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7617708)
- Diagnosed-only studies show a higher male to female ratio (about 4.56:1) than high-quality active screening studies (about 3.32:1), which is population-level evidence of recognition bias (secondhand figures). Confidence: medium. Source: [Embrace Autism summary of Loomes et al. 2017](https://embrace-autism.com/the-autism-sex-ratio/)

### Inferences
- Caregiver logs are themselves a filtered signal. Expect under-logging of quiet distress in everyone, and more so in girls, older children, and settings where the person is masking. Log frequency should not be read as distress frequency without that caveat.
- Design responses that do not require a sex field: (a) prompt explicitly for quiet signs (went silent, withdrew, stopped eating, complained of stomach ache or headache, froze, hid) alongside visible ones; (b) give quiet events the same weight as loud ones in pattern statistics; (c) capture setting (home, school, community) and who observed, since ratings vary by observer; (d) in eval cases, include paired scripted inputs where the same underlying distress is described for a girl and a boy and check that structured output and any suggestions are equivalent.
- Bias audit for synthetic data: check that the generator does not produce only externalizing events for boys and only internalizing events for girls, since the evidence shows large overlap and weak sex effects.

### Gaps
- Experimental vignette studies (same behavior description, child's sex varied) with parents or teachers were not found in this session. This is the most direct test of rating bias and remains an open question here.
- Two further studies surfaced in search summaries without a confirmed primary URL, so they are listed here and not as findings: Paolizzi et al. 2025 (parents rated girls as more social and less repetitive than boys although ADOS-2 showed no sex difference), and a study of 135 children (girls had higher parent-reported SRS-2 traits across all domains with no ADOS-2 difference). They point in different directions on parent report, which itself signals that the parent-side evidence is mixed.
- No study was found on whether home caregivers log distress events at different rates by the person's sex in diary or app-based data.
- Evidence for adults (ratings by partners, family, or paid caregivers by sex) was not found.
