# Problem

## Target user
Anyone who lives with or cares for someone who cannot reliably say what they need: an adult child caring for a parent living with dementia, a spouse, a sibling, a home aide, or a parent of an autistic child. They are mostly not professionals. They are tired, short on time, and logging from a phone in the moment, often with several people sharing the care.

**Named personas (synthetic):**
- Neha, 48, cares for her mother Helen (79, dementia) at home, with her brother on weekends and a home aide on weekday mornings.
- Priya, parent of Arjun (7, autistic), whose mornings before school often end in meltdowns.

## The problem
When a person living with dementia, or an autistic child, is in pain, hungry, tired, or overwhelmed, they often cannot say so. It comes out as agitation, calling out, refusing care, or meltdowns, and it is easy to treat as a "behavior problem" instead of a need to meet. Care guidance describes these behaviors as a form of communication of unmet needs, often pain.

The clues exist but are scattered. The home aide sees a small lunch in the morning, the daughter sees the agitation in the afternoon, and nobody connects them. Parents keep notes on their phones and still cannot see a pattern or explain it clearly to a doctor or therapist.

## Scale (why it matters)
- 63 million Americans are family caregivers, up 45% from 2015 (AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, Caregiving in the U.S. 2025).
- 7.4 million Americans age 65 and older are estimated to be living with Alzheimer's in 2026, supported by nearly 13 million unpaid caregivers who provided more than 19 billion hours of care in 2025 (Alzheimer's Association, 2026 Facts and Figures).
- About 1 in 31 eight-year-old children in the U.S. has been identified with autism (CDC ADDM Network, 2025).

## Evidence of need (from our users)
One parent so far, on our team, in his own words. The words come from a note he spoke into the app about a hard moment with his daughter the night before, and he agreed to their use. Source and context: `docs/research/caregiver_quotes.md`. This is one family, not an interview study. Interviews with caregivers of a person living with dementia are still NEED DATA.

| Who (role) | What they said | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| A parent on our team, father of an autistic daughter | "I asked her what is happening... she's pointing to the head and she's not liking it, and she was crying." | She could not say what was wrong. He asked, she pointed, and he had to work out the rest from a gesture. |
| The same parent | "She has never done something like this ever before." | A sudden change with no record to compare it against: nothing to show what was different that day or what had helped before. |

## Why AI
- A caregiver's spoken note is messy ("she was really worked up around 3, barely ate"). AI turns it into consistent, countable fields in seconds, which is what makes pattern finding possible at all for someone with no time to fill in forms.
- AI explains patterns in plain caregiver language and catches safety signals (falls, self-injury, caregiver distress) in free speech.
- The patterns and scores themselves are plain statistics, so the AI cannot invent them.

## Why now
- The number of family caregivers has risen sharply and most get little support.
- Speech-to-text in every browser plus reliable structured output from language models make 10-second voice logging possible for the first time without special hardware.
- Families already share care across several people and phones; there is no shared, low-effort record of what each person sees.

## What we are not
Not a diagnostic tool, not medical advice, not a replacement for doctors, therapists, or care teams. Unspoken supports caregivers and never diagnoses.
