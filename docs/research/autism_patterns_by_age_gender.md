# Age shapes autistic distress more than gender

Age band, trigger, and communication level explain far more of how distress shows up in autistic people than sex or gender does, so CareSignal should segment patterns and strategy rankings by age band and trigger and use gender only to audit its own bias. Across the notes, outward distress (meltdown, aggression toward others, elopement) is most common in the youngest children and eases with age, while withdrawal, shutdown, and exhaustion grow through adolescence and dominate adult accounts. Sleep problems are the most common co-occurring issue at every age and the best-supported statistical lever: night waking and irregular sleep track with harder next days. The popular picture that girls internalize and boys externalize is weakly supported; the newest meta-analysis finds no significant sex difference in overall internalizing, and the real gender risk is detection, because quiet distress gets noticed and logged less, and girls are identified later and rated lower by teachers than by parents. Pain and gastrointestinal discomfort can appear as distress behavior, especially in children with limited speech, which supports the app's "contact your doctor or care team" path over any trigger suggestion. On strategies, visual supports, parent-delivered bedtime routines, movement, and trigger reduction have the strongest support in ages 2 to 11; weighted blankets did not improve sleep in a randomized trial, and screens as a calming tool carry a caution. "After-school restraint collapse" is a parenting term with no peer-reviewed validation, so it can seed synthetic data and a "possible thing to check" but cannot be claimed as an established pattern.

**Limitations note (read before using any figure).** The researchers could not open full texts in this session: PubMed, PubMed Central, CDC Stacks, NICE, the NCAEP site, and UCL Discovery were blocked or unreachable. **Every figure below comes from abstracts, repository records, press summaries, or search-result summaries, not from full papers.** Each claim carries a confidence level that reflects both study strength and how well the figure was verified: high (large study, meta-analysis, or guideline seen in more than one source), medium (single study or abstract-level only), low (small, qualitative, practice guidance, or secondhand). Items marked "unverified" were recalled or reported secondhand and need checking before anyone quotes them. No study gives per-age-band rates for meltdown, shutdown, stimming increase, food refusal, or transition distress as logged event types, and none measures distress by time of day. Wherever this report proposes rates, time-of-day shapes, or tier placements for synthetic data, those are our modelling assumptions and are labelled as such. This research informs synthetic data and product design only. Nothing here is diagnostic, and every pattern the app surfaces stays a "possible thing to check", never a cause.

## Outward distress eases with age while quiet distress grows

The clearest age signal is for aggression toward others. In 1,380 autistic children aged 4 to 17, **68% had at some point shown aggression toward a caregiver** and 49% toward others, and aggression was associated with younger age ([Kennedy Krieger IAN summary](https://www.kennedykrieger.org/stories/interactive-autism-network-ian/aggression_and_asd); confidence: high for the figures, medium for generalizability). A registry study of 1,584 children put "physical aggression is a current concern" at **53%, highest in young children**, and linked it with sleep problems, sensory problems, gastrointestinal problems, and self-injury ([SPARK summary of Mazurek et al. 2013](https://sparkforautism.org/discover_article/understanding-aggressive-behavior-in-autism/); confidence: high for the headline, per-band rates not retrieved). On a validated checklist cutoff the figure drops to about **1 in 4** ([Hill et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4160737); confidence: high). The spread from 25% to 68% is a definition effect, and it matters for seeding: a persona that logs "any hitting or kicking" sits near one half, a persona with intense, frequent events sits near one quarter.

For ages 2 to 5 the common belief that limited speech drives meltdowns does not hold up. In 240 autistic children aged 15 to 71 months, IQ, language comprehension, and speech clarity together explained **under 3% of the variance** in how often tantrums (the study's term) occurred, and children with emerging speech had more than children with less speech ([Penn State news on Mayes et al. 2017](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/speech-and-language-deficits-children-autism-may-not-cause-tantrums); confidence: medium to high). Communication level therefore belongs in the profile as a prompt for how to check needs, not as a predictor of how much distress a child has.

From ages 9 to 18 the trajectory splits. Hyperactivity improved most, irritability improved less, and **social withdrawal worsened for a substantial proportion of autistic youths** but not for the comparison group, with more person-to-person variety among autistic youths ([Anderson et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3185304); [The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/teenage-wasteland/); confidence: high for direction, medium for proportions). School becomes a main context in the 6 to 11 band: in a self-selected survey of 947 UK parents, school distress began at a mean of **7.94 years** in autistic children and lasted longer than in non-autistic peers ([Connolly et al. 2023, Frontiers in Psychiatry](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1237052/pdf); confidence: medium, not a population rate). No study found a measured rise in aggression at puberty; that claim rests on practitioner commentary ([Autism Speaks expert opinion](https://autismspeaks.org/expert-opinion/autism-puberty-aggression); confidence: low).

Adults improve on average but describe a different kind of distress. Among 241 autistic people aged 10 to 52 followed for about 4.5 years, most stayed stable, more improved than worsened, adults over 31 showed the fewest distress behaviors, and co-occurring intellectual disability was linked to less improvement ([Washington University release on Shattuck et al. 2007](https://source.washu.edu/2007/09/study-shows-autism-symptoms-can-improve-into-adulthood/); confidence: high). In interviews with 32 autistic adults, meltdowns were described as largely internal and painful, set off by sensory input, social situations, information overload, or unexpected change, usually after stressors had piled up ([Lewis and Stevens 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36632658/); confidence: medium, speaking or writing adults only). Autistic burnout, described from 19 adult interviews plus online posts, means chronic exhaustion, loss of skills, and **reduced tolerance to stimulus, typically lasting 3 months or more**, attributed to life stress plus the cumulative load of masking ([Raymaker et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/); confidence: medium, qualitative). Shutdown is described in guidance as having the same triggers as a meltdown but with the person freezing or switching off ([Autistica](https://www.autistica.org.uk/what-is-autism/supporting-meltdowns); confidence: medium as description, no prevalence data).

Two cautions bound this picture. First, where intellectual disability co-occurs, outward distress persists: in 417 children with severe intellectual disability, aggression, property damage, and self-injury persisted in 69%, 57%, and 58% over 15 to 18 months ([Davies and Oliver 2016](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/27171542/Davies_Oliver_2016_Self_injury_aggression_and_destruction_in_SID_RDD.pdf); confidence: medium to high, not autism-specific). Second, masking, shutdown, and burnout are documented almost only in speaking people without intellectual disability, because those are the people the qualitative studies sampled ([Lewis and Stevens 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36632658/); confidence: high). Quiet distress in people with limited speech is unmeasured, not absent.

## Triggers hold steady across ages while settings and load change

The same core triggers appear at every age: sensory differences, change in routine, anxiety, and communication difficulty ([National Autistic Society, Meltdowns](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/behaviour/meltdowns/all-audiences); confidence: medium, expert consensus). The Autism Society adds unclear instructions, unpredictable waiting, and crowded spaces ([Autism Society guide, 2025](https://autismsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/AutismSociety_Autistic-Meltdowns-Shutdowns_2025-06V2F_Digital.pdf); confidence: medium), and Raising Children Network advises a diary of time of day, sensory input, tiredness, hunger, and the activity just before ([Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/behaviour/common-concerns/aggressive-behaviour-asd); confidence: medium, guidance). That diary is close to what CareSignal already captures by voice. **No study ranks triggers by frequency within age bands**, and screen time ending, hunger, waiting, and denied requests appear only in guidance-level sources. They are plausible and widely reported, not quantified.

Sensory differences do not fade. Unusual sensory reactivity is reported in **69% to about 95%** of autistic children ([Elwin et al.](https://mijn.bsl.nl/sensory-clusters-of-adults-with-and-without-autism-spectrum-cond/11236216); confidence: high for the range, secondary citation), and 221 autistic adults reported more over-responsivity than 181 non-autistic adults across every sense ([CiNii record of Tavassoli et al. 2014](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699993770078464); confidence: medium to high). A meta-analysis is often cited as showing sensory features peaking around ages 6 to 9 ([summary at PMC5592014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5592014); confidence: medium to low, and the mapping of this statement to this URL is unverified). Longitudinal work mostly shows stability, and the peak coincides with entry into noisy classrooms, so setting explains it as well as biology does.

Time of day is the largest gap for a pattern-finding app. A targeted search found no diary or momentary-assessment study comparing distress across morning, after-school, and bedtime windows ([family diary study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10915142/); confidence: medium that the gap is real). Guidance names mornings before school, the after-school window, and bedtime as high-load periods, which is why it tells caregivers to record time of day ([National Autistic Society](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/behaviour/meltdowns/all-audiences); confidence: medium).

**"After-school restraint collapse" has no peer-reviewed validation.** The term was coined by a counsellor and parenting educator, is not a clinical term, and no study measuring its timing, frequency, or prevalence was found ([NYU "The Nest Egg" blog](https://wp.nyu.edu/thenestegg/tag/masking); confidence: high that the term is non-clinical, medium on attribution). The support is indirect. Charity guidance says autistic children often mask at school and have meltdowns or shutdowns at home ([Middletown Centre for Autism](https://www.middletownautism.com/social-media/after-school-restraint-collapse-9-2021); confidence: medium as a description of reports). A study of 8 autistic adolescent girls found exhaustion to be the main cost of camouflaging at school, with one girl describing more anger at home afterward ([Halsall et al. 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13623613211012819); confidence: low to medium). In 388 sex-matched autistic youth, parents of girls reported more autistic traits than teachers did, and the parent versus teacher gap widened with age ([Putnam et al. 2024](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/e/mdl-39060706); confidence: medium; the masking explanation is the authors' interpretation). Evidence in boys, in primary-age children, and in children with intellectual disability is essentially absent. CareSignal can let its statistics find a weekday afternoon cluster in a family's own logs and report it as a count. It cannot name the pattern as an established phenomenon.

## Sleep, elopement, pain, and food change shape by age band

**Sleep is the most common co-occurring issue and the best-supported statistical signal.** About 53% of autistic children aged 2 to 5 had at least one frequent sleep problem, against 32% of typically developing children ([Krakowiak et al. 2008](https://read.qxmd.com/read/18482108/sleep-problems-in-children-with-autism-spectrum-disorders-developmental-delays-and-typical-development-a-population-based-study); confidence: high). Parents reported significant sleep problems in **71% of children aged 4 to 10**, a figure the authors treat as an upper-range estimate because of the screening cutoff ([MDedge summary of Malow et al. 2016](https://mdedge.com/content/sleep-problems-common-among-youth-asd); confidence: medium). The type shifts with age: bedtime resistance, sleep anxiety, and night waking in younger children; delayed sleep onset, short sleep, and daytime sleepiness in older children and adolescents ([Goldman et al. 2012](https://mijn.bsl.nl/parental-sleep-concerns-in-autism-spectrum-disorders-variations-/552366); confidence: high for the pattern, per-band percentages not retrieved). Autistic adults sleep worse than non-autistic adults on quality, efficiency, and time to fall asleep ([Morgan et al. 2020](https://scinapse.io/papers/2966550280); confidence: high for direction, no prevalence figure).

The link to the next day is real but correlational. In 81 autistic children, **night waking had the most consistent association** with daytime aggression and irritability ([ScienceDaily on Mazurek and Sohl 2016](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/02/160216143912.htm); confidence: medium). Across more than 20,000 nights from 67 autistic people with high support needs in residential settings, the previous night's sleep predicted next-day distress behavior better than chance for **81% of individuals**, and night-to-night variability predicted better than hours slept ([Cohen et al. 2018](https://read.qxmd.com/read/29197172/sleep-patterns-predictive-of-daytime-challenging-behavior-in-individuals-with-low-functioning-autism); confidence: medium to high for that population; extending it to children at home is our inference). The honest product sentence is "harder days more often followed nights with waking", never "poor sleep caused the meltdown".

**Elopement is a young-child safety issue first.** In a parent survey covering 1,218 autistic children, 49% had eloped at least once after age 4; the rate was **46% at ages 4 to 7 and 27% at ages 8 to 11** ([ScienceDaily on Anderson et al. 2012](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/10/121008082650.htm); [Disability Scoop](https://disabilityscoop.com/?p=16601); confidence: high, self-selected sample). Among children who went missing, 65% had a close call with traffic and 24% with drowning ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/nearly-half-of-children-with-autism-wander-from-families); confidence: high; the denominator is children who went missing). In a nationally representative survey, 26.7% of children aged 6 to 17 with autism, intellectual disability, or developmental delay had wandered in the past year, more at 6 to 11 than at 12 to 17 ([Kiely et al. 2016](https://doaj.org/article/6915cfcc0b97479a86258d6d3da105b8); confidence: high). Drowning, suffocation, and asphyxiation made up 79.4% of injury deaths in autistic children on US death certificates ([Guan and Li 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388960/); confidence: high, with the caveat that this is a share-of-deaths measure, not an absolute risk). No reliable sex difference was found ([commentary on Kiely et al.](https://questioning-answers.blogspot.ca/2016/02/wandering-and-autism-continued.html); confidence: low).

**Self-injury is common and has no dependable age or sex gradient.** Pooled prevalence is 42% across 37 papers, with hitting oneself by hand the most common form at 23% ([Steenfeldt-Kristensen et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7557528/); confidence: high), and 27.7% in records of 8,065 autistic 8-year-olds ([Soke et al. 2016](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/45225); confidence: high, likely an undercount). The meta-analysis found higher prevalence in females; a large two-sample study found no sex association ([The Transmitter on Soke et al. 2017](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/large-study-shows-self-injury-common-among-children-autism/); confidence in any sex effect: low). It persisted in 44% of 67 people over 10 years ([Laverty et al. 2020](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/persistence-and-predictors-of-self-injurious-behaviour-in-autism-); confidence: medium).

**Pain and gastrointestinal discomfort can look like distress behavior.** Autistic children have about four times the odds of any gastrointestinal symptom ([McElhanon et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24777214/); confidence: high), with constipation in 37% in an updated meta-analysis ([Gan et al. 2023](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fped.2023.1120728/full); confidence: medium to high) and chronic problems in 24% of 2,973 children aged 2 to 17 ([Mazurek et al. 2013](https://mijn.bsl.nl/anxiety-sensory-over-responsivity-and-gastrointestinal-problems-/540900); confidence: high). A consensus panel states that distress behavior can be the primary or sole sign of an underlying medical condition and that support at home complements but does not replace medical evaluation ([Buie et al. 2010 record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/evaluation-diagnosis-and-treatment-of-gastrointestinal-disorders-/); confidence: high as consensus). NICE says to assess physical causes, naming pain and gastrointestinal disorders, before anything else ([NICE QS51 statement 7](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs51/chapter/Quality-statement-7-Assessing-possible-triggers-for-behaviour-that-challenges); confidence: high). One clinic sample shows an age split worth encoding: at ages 2 to 5 upper gastrointestinal symptoms sat alongside aggression, while at ages 6 to 18 they sat alongside worry and body complaints ([Ferguson et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00194); confidence: medium). The same literature warns that these behavioral markers are frequent in children with and without gastrointestinal problems, so they screen poorly ([Maenner et al. 2012](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10803-011-1379-6); confidence: high). That is the case for a care team prompt and against any inferred cause.

Feeding problems are about five times more likely in autistic children than in peers ([Emory news on Sharp et al. 2013](https://news.emory.edu/stories/2013/02/autism_nutritional_deficits/index.html); confidence: high), and about 11% of autistic people in pooled studies meet criteria for a restrictive eating condition that is not strongly female-skewed ([Sader et al. 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11891632/); confidence: medium, wide interval). Long-standing selective eating is a need to work with. A sudden new refusal of food or drink is different and belongs on the red-flag path. Finally, epilepsy affects about 12.5% of autistic children aged 2 to 17 and 26% by age 13 and older ([Viscidi et al. 2013](https://doaj.org/article/30c78a374d1842959a43c46016eca262); confidence: high), which supports treating any sudden major change as a red flag at every age, and most of all in teens.

## Gender changes who gets noticed more than what distress looks like

The sex ratio in identification is closer to 3 to 1 than 4 to 1. CDC surveillance for 2022 found **3.4 boys identified per girl at age 8** ([CDC MMWR, Shaw et al. 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/ss/ss7402a1.htm); confidence: high), and a meta-analysis of 54 studies concluded the ratio among children meeting criteria is nearer 3 to 1 ([Loomes et al. 2017 record](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1558343); confidence: high). Girls are identified later: nearly 1.5 years later in one regional cohort ([Neuroscience News on RI-CART](https://neurosciencenews.com/asd-female-cooccurring-disorders-15538); confidence: medium), and in a health-records analysis one in four women against about one in eight men were first identified at 19 or older ([ABC News on Epic Research](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/news/story/autism-diagnoses-happening-earlier-boys-girls-wait-years-124586710); confidence: medium to low, not peer reviewed). At equal trait levels, girls who were identified had more additional difficulties than girls who were not, with no such gap in boys ([The Transmitter on Dworzynski et al. 2012](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/diagnosis-eludes-many-girls-with-autism-study-says/); confidence: medium to high). Datasets built from identified girls therefore over-represent visible distress, and a caregiver app must not require a diagnosis.

**The "girls internalize, boys externalize" picture is weak.** A 2025 meta-analysis of 56 studies and 13,410 autistic children and young people found no significant sex difference in overall internalizing (g = 0.10, 95% CI -0.04 to 0.23) or depression, and only more anxiety in girls, clearest in older girls and those with higher IQ ([Suominen et al. 2025 record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10216243); confidence: medium to high, high heterogeneity). Sex differences in externalizing are typically not found in autistic samples ([journal listing](https://antoniusacademie.mijn.bsl.nl/sex-differences-in-externalizing-and-internalizing-symptoms-in-a/17817744); confidence: medium). Sensory differences and insistence on sameness do not differ by sex at the meta-analytic level, while visible stereotyped movements are somewhat more common in boys ([Edwards et al. 2023](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/480499/); confidence: medium to high). Sensory and routine-change triggers are therefore sex-neutral.

Masking differs on average, with large overlap. Autistic women scored higher than autistic men on masking and assimilation but not compensation ([Hull et al. 2020](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10080352/1/Hull%20AAM%20Paper%20proof3609.pdf); confidence: medium to high). Of 18 studies examining sex or gender, 12 suggested females camouflage more and none found males camouflage more ([Cook et al. 2021](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/camouflaging-in-autism-a-systematic-review/); confidence: medium). Camouflaging correlates moderately with anxiety, depression, and social anxiety across 16 pooled studies ([Khudiakova et al. 2024](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-mental-health-outcomes-a/); confidence: medium to high for association, low for causation). Boys mask too, and nearly all of this data comes from adults who can self-report.

Two body-related needs are documented. In a survey of 123 autistic and 114 non-autistic people who have periods, autistic respondents reported that **periods amplified sensory differences and made regulating emotion harder**, and they wanted plain advance information before the first period ([Steward et al. 2018](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050187/); confidence: medium, convenience sample). Parents of 9 minimally verbal autistic girls reported that puberty mostly went well despite their worry beforehand ([Cummins et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7308246); confidence: low to medium). No effect size for cycle-linked change was found. School-age autistic girls also showed more sleep problems than autistic boys in one study ([Estes et al. 2022](https://www.pubmed.gov/36341856/); confidence: medium; other studies are mixed).

A binary field would also misdescribe a real minority. Transgender and gender-diverse adults were **3 to 6 times as likely** as cisgender adults to report an autism diagnosis across 641,860 participants ([The Transmitter on Warrier et al. 2020](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/largest-study-to-date-confirms-overlap-between-autism-and-gender-diversity/); confidence: medium to high for direction, medium for size). No evidence was found on how distress differs for nonbinary or transgender autistic people, so no logic can responsibly branch on it.

## The best-supported strategies are visual, routine, and movement supports

The strongest framework is the NCAEP 2020 review of 972 articles, which lists 28 evidence-based practice categories for ages 0 to 22, among them visual supports, antecedent (trigger-reducing) changes, parent-implemented approaches, augmentative and alternative communication, exercise and movement, and social narratives ([FPG executive summary](https://fpg.unc.edu/publications/executive-summary-evidence-based-practices-children-youth-and-young-adults-autism); confidence: high). Visual supports rest on 104 single-case studies and 2 group studies ([AFIRM brief packet](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Visual-Supports-Brief-Packet.pdf); confidence: high). Exercise reduced stereotyped movement with an effect of g = 0.51 under a conservative model across 22 studies, and age did not change the effect ([Teh et al. 2022](https://mijn.bsl.nl/effects-of-physical-exercise-interventions-on-stereotyped-motor-/19346472); confidence: medium to high). Parent sleep education improved time to fall asleep in 80 children aged 2 to 10, while a leaflet alone did not ([Malow et al. 2014](https://emmes.com/publications/14139); [Vanderbilt news](https://news.vumc.org/2012/11/01/studies-explore-best-ways-to-manage-insomnia-in-autism/); confidence: medium). That contrast is CareSignal's opening: consistent application of a routine helps, and information alone does not.

Popular strategies are weaker than their reputation. **A weighted blanket did not lengthen sleep, shorten time to fall asleep, or reduce night waking** in a placebo-controlled crossover trial of 67 analysed children, although children and parents preferred it ([Gringras et al. 2014](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/76682); confidence: high). Weighted vests are not an evidence-based practice and carry a possible injury risk ([Taylor et al. 2017](https://brcasd.binghamton.edu/?p=290); [ASAT synopsis](https://asatonline.org/research-treatment/research-synopses/the-use-and-the-efficacy-of-weighted-vests/); confidence: high and medium). The largest trial of social stories, in 249 children, found no significant effect on its primary outcome ([ASSSIST-2](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/216811/); confidence: high). A 2025 review found insufficient evidence that the visual schedule itself is what improves transitions ([Mouzakes and Thompson 2025](https://digitalcommons.wne.edu/casfacpubs/10/); confidence: medium), and schedules alone did not help three boys whose distress was about losing a preferred item ([AASEP study](https://www.aasep.org/article/view/349); confidence: medium). Countdown timers help some children and raise anxiety in others ([Autism New Zealand](https://autismnz.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Screen-time-1.pdf); confidence: low). Ear defenders lowered physiological arousal in 6 children aged 8 to 16 ([Pfeiffer et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31798424/); confidence: low to medium). Quiet space, low-arousal responding, after-school decompression, a snack or drink, energy accounting, and period preparation have no trials at all; they rest on guidance and autistic adults' accounts ([Raymaker et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/); confidence: low to medium).

On screens, the caution is specific. In 422 children aged 3 to 5 from the general population, frequent use of devices to calm a child was associated with more emotional dysregulation later ([AAP Q and A](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/autism-media-and-emotion-regulation/); confidence: medium, correlational, not autism-specific). The concern is screens as the routine response to distress, not screens as such. Autistic teens and adults also describe eye contact as aversive and overloading ([Trevisan et al. 2017](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/19017); confidence: medium, qualitative), and autistic adults caution against teaching masking ([Raymaker et al. 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32851204/); confidence: low to medium).

Fit by age follows ownership of the support. Published evidence concentrates in ages 2 to 11 and adult evidence is mostly qualitative, so the reasoning that supports move from adult-led (2 to 5), to shared (6 to 11), to chosen by the young person (12 to 17), to self-directed (adult) is developmental logic from the researchers, not a tested matrix. **No study tested whether any home strategy works differently for girls and boys.** What differs by gender is need (masking load, periods), not strategy effect.

## Product implications for CareSignal

The evidence points to one design rule: **age band, trigger, and communication level drive logic; gender drives nothing except bias audits.** Everything below is a recommendation for the PM. Changes to shared types and frozen contracts go through the PM, and each accepted choice needs one line in `docs/DECISIONS.md`.

### 1. Caregiver profile intake inputs

Replace "mild, moderate, severe" with caregiver-reported support-needs wording. Four reasons support this. It asks what the caregiver actually knows (how much help the person needs day to day), not a clinical rating the app has no standing to collect, which keeps intake non-diagnostic. A "mild" label misdescribes people who mask: youths with less pronounced autism features became markedly more withdrawn over ages 9 to 18 ([The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/teenage-wasteland/); confidence: medium), so "mild" would steer the app to expect less distress exactly where it is hardest to see. The data model already uses "1 mild, 2 moderate, 3 severe" for event intensity, so reusing the words for a person invites confusion in prompts and statistics. Finally, the three support phrases parallel the level descriptors in the current diagnostic manual, which families who have a diagnosis will recognize (unverified: from general knowledge, not checked in this research session).

| Field | Required | Options | Question wording | Used for | Never used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `ageBand` | Yes | `2_5`, `6_11`, `12_17`, `adult` | "How old is the person you care for?" (2 to 5, 6 to 11, 12 to 17, 18 or older) | Segmenting community statistics and Winning Patterns; choosing which optional questions and prompts appear | Assuming which distress types a person has; hiding any event type |
| `gender` | No | `girl_or_woman`, `boy_or_man`, `nonbinary`, `self_described` (free text), `prefer_not_to_say` | "What is their gender? You can skip this." | Bias audits only (for example, checking quiet distress is logged at similar rates across groups); display wording if the caregiver wants it | Any branching of prompts, structured output, suggestions, red flags, or strategy rankings; inferring periods; segmenting community statistics |
| `supportLevel` | Yes | `some_support`, `substantial_support`, `very_substantial_support` | "On most days, how much support do they need?" Some support (manages much of the day with help at harder moments). Substantial support (needs help through much of the day). Very substantial support (needs help with most things, most of the time). | Realistic synthetic personas; optional cohort matching once 5 or more families share the cell; bias audits | Predicting how much distress there "should" be; down-weighting quiet logs for "some support"; any diagnostic statement |
| `communicationLevel` | Yes | `speaks_in_sentences`, `some_words`, `mostly_non_speaking` | "How do they usually communicate?" Speaks in sentences. Uses some words or short phrases. There is no medication field and no diagnosis field, and the app works without a diagnosis. Sleep and periods are the two extras with the best evidence; `daySetting` and `tendsToWander` are lower priority if the feature freeze is close.

### 2. Controlled-vocabulary gaps

The current vocabulary is strong on loud events and thin on quiet ones, on setting, and on the tags the age and gender evidence needs. The table lists proposed additions. All of these are shared-type changes for the PM to approve.

| List | Missing tag | Why the evidence supports it |
|---|---|---|
| eventType | `withdrawal` (went quiet, hid, stayed in room, stopped joining in) | Social withdrawal worsens with age for many autistic youths ([Anderson et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3185304); confidence: high for direction). `shutdown` alone will not catch the slower, lower-key version caregivers describe. | environment | `home`, `public_place`, `work_or_day_program`, `near_water_or_road` | Almost three quarters of elopement was from a home ([CHOP Research in Action](https://injury.research.chop.edu/blog/posts/wandering-kids-autism-spectrum-disorders); confidence: medium), and water and traffic are the main dangers. One existing tag needs a PM look: `medication_time` sits in contextBefore. It is a context, not a strategy, but in autism mode it is the only place medication appears, and the guardrail says no medication anywhere. Hiding it in autism mode is the lower-risk choice.

The evidence supports these red-flag rules. They extend the existing list and keep the existing order: red-flag message first, anything else after.

| Rule | Message | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|

No guideline sets numeric thresholds for a consumer app, so any "after N nights" rule is a product decision and should be recorded as one.

### 4. Segmenting and seeding Winning Pattern rankings

Segment by age band and pattern key (eventType plus context tag), never by sex or gender. No study shows a strategy working differently by sex, most trials have male-majority samples, and gender cells would often fall under the 5-family display rule anyway. Period preparation is the one need-specific strategy, and it keys off `hasPeriods`, not gender. Trigger match should carry more weight than age in the synthetic model (ear defenders for `loud_noise`, a snack for `skipped_meal`, a schedule for `transition`); this is a modelling choice consistent with the finding that schedules alone did not help when distress was about losing a preferred item.

The tiers below are proposed seed targets. Tier labels come from `docs/SCORING.md`, where tier depends on family count, score, and durability, so "plausible tier" means what the seeded numbers should be tuned to produce.

| Strategy | Pattern key and band | Plausible tier | `researchBacked` | Evidence and caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Visual schedule or first-then board | `transition_distress\|transition`, `transition_distress\|routine_change`; 2 to 5, 6 to 11 | Winning pattern, research-backed | Yes | Visual supports are an evidence-based practice ([AFIRM](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Visual-Supports-Brief-Packet.pdf); confidence: high). Seed weak results for `screen_time_ended` and `denied_request`. |
| Consistent wind-down and bedtime routine | `sleep_trouble\|bedtime`, `meltdown\|poor_sleep`; 2 to 5, 6 to 11 | Winning pattern, research-backed | Yes | Parent sleep education improved sleep onset at ages 2 to 10 ([Malow et al. 2014](https://emmes.com/publications/14139); confidence: medium) |
| Movement break | `stimming_increase\|school`, `meltdown\|after_school`; all child bands | Winning or Promising | Yes | Exercise and movement is an evidence-based practice ([Teh et al. 2022](https://mijn.bsl.nl/effects-of-physical-exercise-interventions-on-stereotyped-motor-/19346472); confidence: medium to high); the measured outcome was stereotyped movement, not meltdowns |

Three corrections to the current seed targets in `docs/SCORING.md` follow from this. First, "Weighted blanket at bedtime, about 9 families, Promising" contradicts the one good trial; reseed it with a drop near zero. That exposes a tier gap: a strategy with 5 or more families, a score under 50, and a non-negative drop has no label today, so the PM should decide between a "Mixed results" label and seeding it under 5 families. Second, "Visual morning schedule" is seeded as the winner for `meltdown|poor_sleep`, but schedules fit transition and routine-change triggers; a consistent bedtime routine is the evidence-matched winner for that key, and the visual schedule should lead a transition key. Third, "Five-minute transition warnings" can stay Winning on family logs but must not carry the research-backed badge. Weighted vests should not be in the library at all, and punishment, removing comfort items, and anything that restricts a person never appear as entries, not even as "not recommended".

For plausible numbers, the researchers suggest per-use "helped" rates of roughly 45% to 60% for the top group, 35% to 55% for the middle groups, and a weighted blanket at 15% to 25% on sleep outcome despite being well liked. **These rates are modelling assumptions only; no source reports family-level helpfulness rates.** Published single-case studies over-represent successes, so synthetic numbers should stay modest with wide spread between families.

Wording cautions for strategy cards: keep "Based on family logs, not a clinical trial. Discuss with your care team." on every card; say "research-backed" only where a review or trial supports that strategy for that kind of trigger, and never imply the research tested CareSignal's ranking; describe screens as "Screens can calm in the moment, but families often report a harder time when the screen ends. A possible thing to check is how screen time ends."; describe the weighted blanket as "Many children like it. It did not improve sleep in a trial."; for ages 12 and up, phrase strategies as things to agree with the young person; never write that a strategy "works for girls" or "works for boys"; never describe a strategy as stopping or correcting a behavior, only as meeting a need or easing a trigger.

### 6. What we can and cannot claim in the pitch

We can claim, with the stated sources and confidence levels:

- Sleep problems affect roughly half of autistic preschoolers and are reported for about 7 in 10 children aged 4 to 10, and nights with waking are associated with harder next days (association, not cause).
- About half of autistic children in a large parent survey had eloped at least once after age 4, with the highest rate at ages 4 to 7, which is why elopement triggers a safety message.
- Distress behavior can be the only visible sign of pain or illness in a person with limited speech, which is why red flags come before any pattern.
- Girls are identified later and are rated lower by teachers than by parents, so CareSignal names quiet distress as a first-class event and never branches on gender.
- Visual supports, movement, and consistent bedtime routines have research support in children; our rankings add what worked in family logs and say so on every card.
- A weighted blanket did not improve sleep in a randomized trial, and our rankings reflect that instead of repeating popular advice.

We cannot claim:

- That any CareSignal pattern, rate, or ranking is real-world data. All of it is synthetic, seeded from published figures plus our own stated assumptions.
- That "after-school restraint collapse" is a validated or measured phenomenon.
- Any time-of-day finding from research. None exists; the time bands in our seed data are assumptions.
- That boys and girls show different distress, or that any strategy works better for one gender.
- That any strategy is clinically proven to reduce meltdowns, or that the app identifies causes, pain, seizures, or any condition.
- Any figure in this report as verified against a full paper. Every number came from an abstract or summary and should be rechecked before it goes on a slide.
